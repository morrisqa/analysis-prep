    Section 2 Module 7: Companion Reading and Study Guide

    This guide accompanies three sections of Zorn’s Understanding Real Analysis. Section 4.1 (“Derivatives”) introduces the formal limit definition and establishes differentiability as a condition stronger than continuity. Section 4.2 (“Differentiation Rules”) develops the standard rules as theorems proved from the definition. Section 4.3 (“The Mean Value Theorem”) builds from Rolle’s Theorem to the MVT and applies it to prove monotonicity results and Taylor’s theorem.
    🔗
    Read each section before working its questions. The theme running through all three sections is that every statement about derivatives is ultimately a statement about limits, and every proof uses the limit machinery from Modules 5 and 6. When you encounter a rule you learned mechanically in calculus, ask: which limit law is doing the work here?
    🔗

    Section 4.1: The Derivative (Zorn pp. 163–172).

    Read Zorn’s definition of differentiability at a point carefully. The difference quotient \((f(x) - f(a))/(x - a)\) is a function of \(x\text{;}\) the derivative is the limit of this function as \(x \to a\text{.}\) Make sure you can translate between the \(x \to a\) form and the \(h \to 0\) form: \(f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} (f(a+h) - f(a))/h\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1. The Difference Quotient.

    Write the difference quotient for \(f\) at a point \(a\) in both the \(h \to 0\) and the \(x \to a\) form. Then, for \(f(x) = x^2\text{,}\) compute the difference quotient at \(a = 3\) in simplified form and evaluate its limit.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The two forms are \((f(a+h) - f(a))/h\) as \(h \to 0\) and \((f(x) - f(a))/(x - a)\) as \(x \to a\text{.}\) For \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(a = 3\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \frac{9 + 6h + h^2 - 9}{h} = \frac{6h + h^2}{h} = 6 + h \to 6 \quad \text{as } h \to 0. \end{equation*}
    So \(f'(3) = 6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2. Differentiability Implies Continuity.

    1. State the definition of differentiability of \(f\) at a point \(a\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Prove: if \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\text{,}\) then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\text{.}\) Write the proof in full. The key algebraic step is to write \(f(x) - f(a) = \dfrac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \cdot (x - a)\) and apply the product rule for limits as \(x \to a\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) if \(\lim_{h \to 0} (f(a+h) - f(a))/h\) exists (as a finite real number); its value is called \(f'(a)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Suppose \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\text{.}\) For \(x \neq a\text{,}\) write
    \begin{equation*} f(x) - f(a) = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \cdot (x - a). \end{equation*}
    As \(x \to a\text{,}\) the first factor tends to \(f'(a)\) (by differentiability) and the second factor tends to \(0\text{.}\) By the product rule for limits, \(f(x) - f(a) \to f'(a) \cdot 0 = 0\text{,}\) so \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)\text{,}\) confirming continuity. \(\square\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3. The Sum Rule from the Definition.

    Prove from the limit definition that \((f + g)'(a) = f'(a) + g'(a)\) (the sum rule), assuming both \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at \(a\text{.}\) Your proof must explicitly write the difference quotient for \(f + g\text{,}\) split it into two separate difference quotients, and invoke the sum rule for limits.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The difference quotient for \(f + g\) at \(a\) is
    \begin{align*} \frac{(f+g)(a+h) - (f+g)(a)}{h} \amp= \frac{[f(a+h) - f(a)] + [g(a+h) - g(a)]}{h}\\ \amp= \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} + \frac{g(a+h) - g(a)}{h}. \end{align*}
    As \(h \to 0\text{,}\) the first term tends to \(f'(a)\) and the second tends to \(g'(a)\text{,}\) by differentiability of \(f\) and \(g\) at \(a\text{.}\) By the sum rule for limits, \((f+g)'(a) = f'(a) + g'(a)\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Section 4.2: Differentiation Rules (Zorn pp. 172–182).

    Section 4.2 develops the product rule, quotient rule, and chain rule as theorems. The product rule proof is a model of the “add and subtract a clever term” technique that appears repeatedly in analysis. The chain rule proof requires more care because of a subtle division-by-zero issue.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.4. The Product Rule.

    State the product rule: \((fg)'(a) = f'(a)g(a) + f(a)g'(a)\text{.}\) Then prove it from the limit definition. The key step is to add and subtract the term \(f(a+h)g(a)\) in the numerator of the difference quotient for \(fg\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    The difference quotient for \(fg\) at \(a\) is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{f(a+h)g(a+h) - f(a)g(a)}{h}. \end{equation*}
    Add and subtract \(f(a+h)g(a)\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \amp= \frac{f(a+h)g(a+h) - f(a+h)g(a) + f(a+h)g(a) - f(a)g(a)}{h}\\ \amp= f(a+h) \cdot \frac{g(a+h) - g(a)}{h} + g(a) \cdot \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}. \end{align*}
    As \(h \to 0\text{:}\) \(f(a+h) \to f(a)\) (since \(f\) is continuous at \(a\text{,}\) by differentiability), the second factor tends to \(g'(a)\text{,}\) and \(g(a)\) is a constant times a factor tending to \(f'(a)\text{.}\) By the sum and product rules for limits, \((fg)'(a) = f(a)g'(a) + g(a)f'(a)\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5. The Chain Rule and Its Proof.

    1. State the chain rule: if \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) and \(g\) is differentiable at \(f(a)\text{,}\) then \((g \circ f)'(a) = g'(f(a)) \cdot f'(a)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Explain in words why the “obvious” proof attempt—multiply and divide by \(f(a+h) - f(a)\)—has a gap.
      🔗
    3. Describe the auxiliary function approach that fixes the gap. Define \(\psi(k) = (g(f(a)+k) - g(f(a)))/k - g'(f(a))\) for \(k \neq 0\) and \(\psi(0) = 0\text{,}\) and explain why this function is continuous at \(0\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) The chain rule statement is given. (b) The obvious approach writes
    \begin{equation*} \frac{g(f(a+h)) - g(f(a))}{h} = \frac{g(f(a+h)) - g(f(a))}{f(a+h) - f(a)} \cdot \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}, \end{equation*}
    intending to take limits to get \(g'(f(a)) \cdot f'(a)\text{.}\) The gap: \(f(a+h) - f(a)\) might equal \(0\) for arbitrarily small \(h \neq 0\text{,}\) making the first fraction undefined at those values of \(h\text{.}\) (c) The auxiliary function \(\psi\) rewrites \(g(f(a)+k) - g(f(a)) = (g'(f(a)) + \psi(k)) k\) for all \(k\text{,}\) including \(k = 0\text{.}\) Substituting \(k = f(a+h) - f(a)\) gives a valid expression with no division by zero. Since \(g\) is differentiable at \(f(a)\text{,}\) \(\psi(k) \to 0\) as \(k \to 0\text{,}\) and \(\psi\) is continuous at \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.6. Applying the Rules.

    Apply the product and chain rules to differentiate each function. State which rule you use at each step.
    1. \(\displaystyle f(x) = x^2 \sin(x)\)
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle g(x) = (x^2 + 1)^{10}\)
      🔗
    3. \(h(x) = \ln(x^2 + 1)\text{,}\) assuming \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) Product rule: \(f'(x) = 2x\sin(x) + x^2\cos(x)\text{.}\) (b) Chain rule: \(g'(x) = 10(x^2+1)^9 \cdot 2x = 20x(x^2+1)^9\text{.}\) (c) Chain rule: \(h'(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2+1} \cdot 2x = \dfrac{2x}{x^2+1}\text{.}\)

    Section 4.3: The Mean Value Theorem (Zorn pp. 182–196).

    Section 4.3 is the heart of Module 7. Read the proof of Rolle’s Theorem carefully: it uses the Extreme Value Theorem (Module 6) in an essential way. The MVT proof derives from Rolle’s by a geometric trick (subtracting the secant line) that you should be able to reconstruct from memory by the end of this module.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.7. Rolle’s Theorem.

    State Rolle’s Theorem in full (all three hypotheses and the conclusion). Then, for \(f(x) = x^2 - 4x\) on \([0, 4]\text{,}\) verify that all three hypotheses hold and find the value \(c \in (0, 4)\) guaranteed by the conclusion.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Rolle’s Theorem: if \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\text{,}\) differentiable on \((a, b)\text{,}\) and \(f(a) = f(b)\text{,}\) then there exists \(c \in (a, b)\) with \(f'(c) = 0\text{.}\) For \(f(x) = x^2 - 4x\) on \([0, 4]\text{:}\) (i) \(f\) is a polynomial, hence continuous on \([0, 4]\) and differentiable on \((0, 4)\text{.}\) (ii) \(f(0) = 0\) and \(f(4) = 16 - 16 = 0\text{,}\) so \(f(0) = f(4)\text{.}\) By Rolle’s, there exists \(c \in (0,4)\) with \(f'(c) = 0\text{.}\) Since \(f'(x) = 2x - 4\text{,}\) we get \(c = 2 \in (0, 4)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.8. Proof of the MVT from Rolle’s Theorem.

    State the Mean Value Theorem. Then prove it using Rolle’s Theorem by defining the auxiliary function
    \begin{equation*} h(x) = f(x) - \left[f(a) + \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}(x - a)\right]. \end{equation*}
    Verify that \(h(a) = h(b) = 0\text{,}\) apply Rolle’s Theorem to \(h\text{,}\) and interpret the conclusion for \(f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    MVT: If \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and differentiable on \((a, b)\text{,}\) then there exists \(c \in (a, b)\) such that \(f'(c) = (f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof: Define \(h(x) = f(x) - [f(a) + \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a)]\text{.}\) Then \(h\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and differentiable on \((a, b)\) (being a difference of such functions), and \(h(a) = f(a) - f(a) = 0\text{,}\) \(h(b) = f(b) - [f(a) + (f(b)-f(a))] = 0\text{.}\) By Rolle’s Theorem, there exists \(c \in (a, b)\) with \(h'(c) = 0\text{.}\) Since \(h'(x) = f'(x) - (f(b)-f(a))/(b-a)\text{,}\) we get \(f'(c) = (f(b)-f(a))/(b-a)\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.9. MVT Application: Zero Derivative.

    Use the MVT to prove: if \(f'(x) = 0\) for all \(x \in (a, b)\text{,}\) then \(f\) is constant on \((a, b)\text{.}\) (Hint: take any two points \(x, y \in (a, b)\) with \(x \lt y\text{,}\) apply the MVT on \([x, y]\text{,}\) and use \(f'(c) = 0\) for the resulting \(c\text{.}\))
    🔗
    Answer.
    Let \(x, y \in (a, b)\) with \(x \lt y\text{.}\) Since \(f\) is differentiable on \((a, b)\text{,}\) it is continuous on \([x, y]\) and differentiable on \((x, y)\text{.}\) By the MVT, there exists \(c \in (x, y) \subset (a, b)\) such that \(f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)(y - x) = 0 \cdot (y - x) = 0\text{.}\) Hence \(f(y) = f(x)\text{.}\) Since \(x, y \in (a, b)\) were arbitrary, \(f\) is constant on \((a, b)\text{.}\) \(\square\)
