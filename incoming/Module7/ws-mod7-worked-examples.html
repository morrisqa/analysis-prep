    Section 3 Module 7: Worked Examples

    Read each example slowly. The goal is not just to see what the answer is, but to internalize the proof template. Example 1 shows how to compute a derivative directly from the limit definition. Example 2 proves differentiability implies continuity—a result that looks obvious but requires a specific algebraic trick. Example 3 is a full proof of Rolle’s Theorem, the key lemma for the MVT. Example 4 demonstrates the MVT used to produce an inequality, the most common application pattern in MAT 5610.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1. Example 1: Derivative from the Definition.

    Prove that \(f(x) = x^3\) is differentiable at every \(a \in \R\) and that \(f'(a) = 3a^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy.
    🔗
    Compute the difference quotient \((f(a+h) - f(a))/h\text{,}\) simplify the numerator algebraically, cancel the factor of \(h\text{,}\) and evaluate the resulting limit as \(h \to 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Scratch work.
    🔗
    Expand the numerator:
    \begin{equation*} (a+h)^3 - a^3 = a^3 + 3a^2h + 3ah^2 + h^3 - a^3 = 3a^2 h + 3ah^2 + h^3 = h(3a^2 + 3ah + h^2). \end{equation*}
    Dividing by \(h\) leaves \(3a^2 + 3ah + h^2\text{,}\) which tends to \(3a^2\) as \(h \to 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Let \(a \in \R\text{.}\) For \(h \neq 0\text{,}\) compute:
    \begin{align*} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} \amp= \frac{(a+h)^3 - a^3}{h}\\ \amp= \frac{3a^2 h + 3ah^2 + h^3}{h}\\ \amp= 3a^2 + 3ah + h^2. \end{align*}
    By the sum and product rules for limits (Module 6),
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{h \to 0} (3a^2 + 3ah + h^2) = 3a^2 + 0 + 0 = 3a^2. \end{equation*}
    By the definition of the derivative, \(f'(a) = 3a^2\text{.}\) Since \(a \in \R\) was arbitrary, \(f\) is differentiable at every point and \(f'(x) = 3x^2\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice.
    🔗
    The entire work is in the algebra of the numerator: expanding \((a+h)^3 - a^3\) and identifying the factor of \(h\) that cancels the denominator. Once that cancellation is done, the limit is immediate from the limit laws. This is the standard template for every computation of a derivative from the definition: simplify, cancel, then take the limit.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2. Example 2: Differentiability Implies Continuity.

    Prove that if \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\text{,}\) then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy.
    🔗
    Write \(f(x) - f(a)\) as a product involving the difference quotient, then apply the product rule for limits. The key algebraic insight is that we can insert the factor \((x - a)\) in the denominator as long as we also put it in the numerator.
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Suppose \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\text{.}\) For \(x \neq a\text{,}\) we may write
    \begin{equation*} f(x) - f(a) = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \cdot (x - a). \end{equation*}
    As \(x \to a\text{,}\) the first factor tends to \(f'(a)\) by the hypothesis of differentiability, and the second factor tends to \(0\text{.}\) By the product rule for limits,
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) - f(a)] = f'(a) \cdot 0 = 0. \end{equation*}
    Hence \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)\text{,}\) so \(f\) is continuous at \(a\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice.
    🔗
    The key algebraic trick is writing \(f(x) - f(a)\) as a product with the difference quotient. This is valid for \(x \neq a\text{,}\) and the limit as \(x \to a\) only looks at \(x \neq a\) anyway. The theorem is one-directional: continuity does not imply differentiability. The function \(f(x) = |x|\) is continuous at \(0\) but not differentiable there (the left- and right-hand difference quotients both exist but are \(-1\) and \(+1\) respectively, so the limit does not exist).
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3. Example 3: Proof of Rolle’s Theorem.

    Prove Rolle’s Theorem: if \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\text{,}\) differentiable on \((a, b)\text{,}\) and \(f(a) = f(b)\text{,}\) then there exists \(c \in (a, b)\) with \(f'(c) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy.
    🔗
    The Extreme Value Theorem (Module 6) guarantees that \(f\) attains its maximum and minimum on \([a, b]\text{.}\) If \(f\) is constant, we are immediately done. If not, at least one extremum must occur at an interior point \(c \in (a, b)\text{,}\) and at an interior extremum of a differentiable function, the derivative must be zero.
    🔗
    Step 1: Handle the trivial case.
    🔗
    (i) If \(f\) is constant on \([a, b]\text{.}\) Then \(f'(c) = 0\) for every \(c \in (a, b)\text{,}\) and the conclusion holds immediately.
    🔗
    Step 2: Handle the nontrivial case.
    🔗
    (ii) If \(f\) is not constant on \([a, b]\text{.}\) By the Extreme Value Theorem (EVT, Module 6), \(f\) attains its maximum at some point in \([a, b]\text{.}\) Since \(f\) is not constant and \(f(a) = f(b)\text{,}\) the maximum cannot equal \(f(a) = f(b)\) at both endpoints simultaneously; there must be some \(x_0 \in (a, b)\) with \(f(x_0) > f(a) = f(b)\text{.}\) Hence the maximum of \(f\) is attained at some point \(c \in (a, b)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3: Conclude \(f'(c) = 0\) at the interior maximum.
    🔗
    At the interior maximum \(c\text{:}\) for \(h \gt 0\) small, \(f(c + h) \leq f(c)\text{,}\) so \((f(c+h) - f(c))/h \leq 0\text{;}\) taking the right-hand limit, \(f'(c) \leq 0\text{.}\) For \(h \lt 0\) small, \(f(c + h) \leq f(c)\text{,}\) so \((f(c+h) - f(c))/h \geq 0\text{;}\) taking the left-hand limit, \(f'(c) \geq 0\text{.}\) Since both inequalities hold and the derivative exists, \(f'(c) = 0\text{.}\) (A symmetric argument applies if \(f\) attains its minimum at an interior point.) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice.
    🔗
    The proof has two cases; the key one requires \(f\) to be non-constant. The Extreme Value Theorem (from Module 6) is the tool that produces the interior extremum, and differentiability is what forces \(f'(c) = 0\) at that point. The argument for \(f'(c) = 0\) at an interior extremum (comparing left-hand and right-hand difference quotients) is itself a standard template in real analysis.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4. Example 4: An MVT Inequality.

    Use the Mean Value Theorem to prove that \(|\sin(x) - \sin(y)| \leq |x - y|\) for all \(x, y \in \R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy.
    🔗
    Apply the MVT to \(f(t) = \sin(t)\) on the interval between \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) then use the bound \(|\cos(c)| \leq 1\) to control the result. This converts a question about how much \(\sin\) can change into a question about how large its derivative can be.
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Fix \(x, y \in \R\text{.}\) If \(x = y\text{,}\) both sides are \(0\) and the inequality holds trivially. Assume WLOG that \(x \lt y\text{.}\) The function \(f(t) = \sin(t)\) is continuous on \([x, y]\) and differentiable on \((x, y)\text{,}\) with \(f'(t) = \cos(t)\text{.}\) By the Mean Value Theorem, there exists \(c \in (x, y)\) such that
    \begin{equation*} f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)(y - x), \quad \text{i.e.,} \quad \sin(y) - \sin(x) = \cos(c)(y - x). \end{equation*}
    Taking absolute values:
    \begin{equation*} |\sin(y) - \sin(x)| = |\cos(c)| \cdot |y - x| \leq |y - x|, \end{equation*}
    since \(|\cos(c)| \leq 1\) for all \(c \in \R\text{.}\) This gives \(|\sin(x) - \sin(y)| \leq |x - y|\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice.
    🔗
    The MVT converts “how much does \(\sin\) change on \([x, y]\text{?}\)” into “how large is \(|\sin'(c)|\text{?}\)” —and the answer is at most 1. This is the prototype of the MVT inequality template: \(|f(y) - f(x)| = |f'(c)| \cdot |y - x| \leq M|y-x|\) whenever \(|f'| \leq M\text{.}\) This template recurs throughout MAT 5610: in the proof of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, in error bounds for Taylor polynomials, and in the definition of Lipschitz continuity.
