    Section 2 Module 4: Companion Reading and Study Guide

    This guide accompanies four sections of Zorn’s Understanding Real Analysis, which together cover the full story of convergent sequences of real numbers. The weight of the module falls on §2.1 (the definition of convergence) and §2.3 (the Monotone Convergence and Bolzano–Weierstrass theorems); §2.2 (limit laws) is labor-saving technology, and §2.4 (Cauchy sequences) reframes convergence in a form you will see again in MAT 5610.
    🔗
    Read each section before working its questions. The questions are designed to be answered with the text open in front of you; quote definitions and theorem numbers rather than paraphrasing when you can.
    🔗

    Section 2.1: Sequences and Convergence (Zorn pp. 83–95).

    This is the central section of the module. Read Definition 2.1 (convergence) more than once; understanding its quantifier structure is the main learning goal of Video 1 and of the whole module. Pay attention also to Definition 2.2 (bounded, monotone) and Theorem 2.3 (a bounded monotone sequence converges).
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1. Parsing the Definition.

    Write out Zorn’s Definition 2.1 of convergence (\(a_n \to L\)) symbolically, using quantifiers (\(\forall\text{,}\) \(\exists\)) and the absolute value. Then answer:
    1. How many quantifiers appear, and in what order?
      🔗
    2. Which variable is chosen by the adversary (i.e., you do not get to pick it), and which is chosen in response?
      🔗
    3. After a proof of convergence is written, what is the natural functional relationship between the adversary’s choice and the responder’s choice: typically, does \(N\) get larger or smaller as \(\varepsilon\) gets smaller?
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) Three: \(\forall \varepsilon \gt 0\ \exists N \in \N\ \forall n \geq N: |a_n - L| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\) The order is \(\forall\text{,}\) \(\exists\text{,}\) \(\forall\text{.}\) (b) The adversary picks \(\varepsilon\text{;}\) we pick \(N\) in response. The final \(\forall n \geq N\) is a conclusion, not a choice. (c) \(N\) gets larger as \(\varepsilon\) gets smaller: a finer tolerance requires going farther out in the sequence.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2. Negating the Definition.

    Write out the negation of convergence—that is, what \(a_n \not\to L\) means—by applying the standard negation rules from Module 1 (push \(\neg\) through each quantifier in turn).
    🔗
    Then state what it means, in this same format, for the sequence \((a_n)\) to be divergent—that is, for no \(L \in \R\) to be its limit.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(a_n \not\to L\) means: \(\exists \varepsilon_0 \gt 0\ \forall N \in \N\ \exists n \geq N: |a_n - L| \geq \varepsilon_0\text{.}\) Divergence is: \(\forall L \in \R\ \exists \varepsilon_0 \gt 0\ \forall N \in \N\ \exists n \geq N: |a_n - L| \geq \varepsilon_0\text{.}\) The key point is that to disprove convergence to a specific \(L\text{,}\) you exhibit a single \(\varepsilon_0\) that fails for every \(N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3. From Scratch Work to Formal Proof.

    In Zorn’s worked examples in §2.1, the author often first performs algebraic manipulation (finding the relationship between \(n\) and \(\varepsilon\)) and then writes a formal proof. Re-read Zorn’s proof that \(1/n \to 0\) (or whichever first explicit \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) argument Zorn presents near Definition 2.1).
    🔗
    Identify: where in the formal proof does the Archimedean Property appear, and why is it essential? Which of Module 2’s tools is the Archimedean Property a consequence of?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.4. Boundedness.

    State Zorn’s definition of a bounded sequence (Definition 2.2 or nearby). Then prove Theorem 2.4 informally: if \(a_n \to L\text{,}\) then \((a_n)\) is bounded.
    🔗
    (Sketch: apply the definition of convergence with \(\varepsilon = 1\) to bound the tail; then use the finiteness of the first \(N\) terms to bound the head. Write this as two or three complete sentences.)
    🔗
    Answer.
    A sequence is bounded if there exists \(M \gt 0\) such that \(|a_n| \leq M\) for all \(n \in \N\text{.}\) If \(a_n \to L\text{,}\) pick \(N\) so that \(|a_n - L| \lt 1\) for \(n \geq N\text{;}\) then \(|a_n| \leq |L| + 1\) for such \(n\text{.}\) The initial terms \(a_1, \ldots, a_{N-1}\) form a finite set, so \(M = \max\{|a_1|, \ldots, |a_{N-1}|, |L| + 1\}\) bounds the entire sequence.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Section 2.2: Working with Limits (Zorn pp. 96–105).

    Section 2.2 collects the tools that let you evaluate limits of sums, products, quotients, and sandwiched sequences without returning to the definition. The main results are the Algebra of Limits (Theorem 2.5) and the Squeeze Theorem (Theorem 2.6).
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5. The Algebra of Limits.

    Suppose \(a_n \to A\) and \(b_n \to B\text{.}\) State the three most commonly used conclusions from Theorem 2.5 (sum, product, quotient).
    🔗
    For the quotient, what additional hypothesis is needed beyond \(a_n \to A\) and \(b_n \to B\text{,}\) and why is that hypothesis necessary?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Sum: \(a_n + b_n \to A + B\text{.}\) Product: \(a_n b_n \to AB\text{.}\) Quotient: \(a_n / b_n \to A / B\text{,}\) provided \(B \neq 0\) (and implicitly the \(b_n\) are eventually nonzero). The hypothesis \(B \neq 0\) is needed to avoid dividing by 0 both in the limit and, via a tail argument, in the quotient \(a_n/b_n\) for large \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.6. Reading the Proof of the Sum Rule.

    Read Zorn’s proof that \(a_n + b_n \to A + B\text{.}\)
    1. What algebraic identity launches the proof? (It is an application of the Triangle Inequality.)
      🔗
    2. Why is the tolerance split as \(\varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2\) instead of simply \(\varepsilon\text{?}\)
      🔗
    3. How is \(N\) chosen in terms of the \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) coming from \(a_n \to A\) and \(b_n \to B\) respectively?
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) \(|(a_n + b_n) - (A + B)| \leq |a_n - A| + |b_n - B|\text{.}\) (b) Each summand is bounded by \(\varepsilon/2\text{,}\) so their sum is bounded by \(\varepsilon\)—the desired bound. (c) \(N = \max(N_1, N_2)\text{,}\) so that both convergence bounds hold simultaneously for \(n \geq N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.7. Using the Algebra of Limits on Rational Expressions.

    Compute
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{5n^2 - 3n}{2n^2 + n + 7}. \end{equation*}
    Do not write an \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) proof. Instead: divide numerator and denominator by \(n^2\text{,}\) identify each term’s limit (using \(1/n \to 0\) and constants), and apply the Algebra of Limits to assemble the answer.
    🔗
    Write your reasoning in complete sentences. Indicate each step at which the algebra of limits is applied.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Dividing gives \(\dfrac{5 - 3/n}{2 + 1/n + 7/n^2}\text{.}\) Since \(1/n \to 0\) and \(1/n^2 = (1/n)(1/n) \to 0 \cdot 0 = 0\) (product rule), we have numerator \(\to 5 - 0 = 5\) (sum rule) and denominator \(\to 2 + 0 + 0 = 2\) (sum rule, twice). Since the denominator’s limit is nonzero, the quotient rule gives \(5/2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.8. The Squeeze Theorem in Practice.

    State the Squeeze Theorem from Zorn §2.2 in your own words. Then apply it to show that \(a_n = \dfrac{\cos(n^2)}{n}\) converges, and identify the limit.
    🔗
    Your solution should explicitly write the two bounding sequences you are squeezing between, explain why each converges (to what), and then invoke the theorem.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(-1 \leq \cos(n^2) \leq 1\text{,}\) so \(-1/n \leq a_n \leq 1/n\) for all \(n \in \N\text{.}\) Both \(-1/n \to 0\) and \(1/n \to 0\) (by Archimedean Property / known limit), so by the Squeeze Theorem \(a_n \to 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Section 2.3: Subsequences and the Big Theorems (Zorn pp. 106–113).

    Section 2.3 introduces subsequences and proves two of the headline theorems of sequential analysis: the Monotone Convergence Theorem and the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem. Both are consequences of completeness; each will be invoked repeatedly in MAT 5610.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.9. Subsequences and Inheritance.

    Define subsequence precisely: what does it mean for \((a_{n_k})\) to be a subsequence of \((a_n)\text{?}\) What is required of the index sequence \(n_1, n_2, \ldots\text{?}\)
    🔗
    State (without proof) the inheritance result: if \(a_n \to L\text{,}\) what is the limit of every subsequence of \((a_n)\text{?}\) State the contrapositive of the inheritance result and explain how it is used to prove divergence.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \((a_{n_k})_{k=1}^{\infty}\) is a subsequence of \((a_n)\) if \(n_1 \lt n_2 \lt n_3 \lt \cdots\) is a strictly increasing sequence in \(\N\text{.}\) Inheritance: if \(a_n \to L\text{,}\) every subsequence also converges to \(L\text{.}\) Contrapositive: if \((a_n)\) has two subsequences converging to different limits, then \((a_n)\) diverges. This is the standard tool for showing that, e.g., \(((-1)^n)\) diverges: the even terms converge to 1 and the odd terms to \(-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.10. Where Completeness Enters the Monotone Convergence Theorem.

    State the Monotone Convergence Theorem as Zorn presents it (Theorem 2.3, or its analogue in your text). Then re-read the proof slowly.
    🔗
    1. What set \(S\) does the proof consider, and why is \(S\) nonempty and bounded above?
      🔗
    2. At what step is the Completeness Axiom invoked, and what object does it produce?
      🔗
    3. How is the \(\varepsilon\)-characterization of the supremum (from Module 2) used to produce the index \(N\) needed by the \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) definition of convergence?
      🔗
    4. Where in the proof is the hypothesis that \((a_n)\) is monotone actually used?
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) \(S = \{a_n : n \in \N\}\text{,}\) the set of terms. It is nonempty (contains \(a_1\)) and bounded above by the given bound on \((a_n)\text{.}\) (b) Completeness is used to assert \(L = \sup S \in \R\text{.}\) (c) The \(\varepsilon\)-characterization provides, for any \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) an index \(N\) with \(a_N \gt L - \varepsilon\text{.}\) (d) Monotonicity (increasingness) is used to pass from \(a_N \gt L - \varepsilon\) to \(a_n \gt L - \varepsilon\) for all \(n \geq N\)—we need to know later terms do not drop back below \(a_N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.11. The Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem.

    State the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem. Read one of Zorn’s proof strategies (whichever he presents; most likely the monotone-subsequence approach).
    🔗
    1. In the monotone-subsequence strategy, the first step is to show every sequence of reals has a monotone subsequence. Why is that claim not obvious?
      🔗
    2. Once a monotone subsequence has been produced, how do the hypotheses of the theorem ensure that the subsequence is bounded?
      🔗
    3. How does Monotone Convergence finish the argument?
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.12. Applying Bolzano–Weierstrass.

    Consider the bounded sequence \(a_n = (-1)^n \cdot \dfrac{n}{n+1}\text{.}\)
    1. Verify that \((a_n)\) is bounded.
      🔗
    2. Bolzano–Weierstrass guarantees a convergent subsequence. Identify one such subsequence explicitly and compute its limit (using limit laws, not \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\)).
      🔗
    3. Find a second convergent subsequence with a different limit. What does this show about \((a_n)\) itself?
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) \(|a_n| = n/(n+1) \lt 1\text{,}\) so \(|a_n| \leq 1\) for all \(n\text{;}\) the sequence is bounded. (b) The even subsequence \(a_{2k} = 2k/(2k+1) \to 1\) (limit laws: divide by \(2k\)). (c) The odd subsequence \(a_{2k-1} = -(2k-1)/(2k) \to -1\text{.}\) Two subsequences with different limits implies \((a_n)\) itself diverges.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Section 2.4: Cauchy Sequences (Zorn pp. 114–120).

    Section 2.4 reframes convergence without reference to a limit. Read Definition 2.17 and Theorem 2.20 carefully. The equivalence of “convergent” and “Cauchy” in \(\R\) is another face of completeness—in \(\Q\) it fails.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.13. The Cauchy Definition.

    State Zorn’s definition of a Cauchy sequence symbolically. Identify every quantifier and its type.
    🔗
    Compare to the definition of convergence. Which variable appears in the convergence definition but is absent from the Cauchy definition? What is the conceptual significance of that absence?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\forall \varepsilon \gt 0\ \exists N \in \N\ \forall m, n \geq N: |a_m - a_n| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\) The absent variable is the limit \(L\text{:}\) the Cauchy definition talks only about the terms of the sequence among themselves. Conceptually, this is what makes Cauchy a “purely internal” notion of convergence, definable without reference to an external candidate limit.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.14. Convergent Implies Cauchy.

    Read Zorn’s proof that every convergent sequence is Cauchy (Proposition 2.18 or its analogue). The core identity is
    \begin{equation*} a_m - a_n = (a_m - L) + (L - a_n). \end{equation*}
    Explain how this identity, combined with the Triangle Inequality and the \(\varepsilon/2\)-split, delivers the Cauchy conclusion.
    🔗
    Is the Completeness Axiom used in this direction of the proof?
    🔗
    Answer.
    By the Triangle Inequality applied to the identity, \(|a_m - a_n| \leq |a_m - L| + |L - a_n|\text{.}\) If \(a_n \to L\text{,}\) pick \(N\) so that \(|a_k - L| \lt \varepsilon/2\) for \(k \geq N\text{;}\) then for \(m, n \geq N\text{,}\) \(|a_m - a_n| \lt \varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2 = \varepsilon\text{.}\) Completeness is not used in this direction: the existence of \(L\) is given by hypothesis.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.15. Cauchy Implies Convergent (where completeness enters).

    Read Zorn’s proof that in \(\R\text{,}\) every Cauchy sequence converges. The argument has three steps:
    🔗
    Step 1: A Cauchy sequence is bounded. Step 2: By Bolzano–Weierstrass, a bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence. Step 3: A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges.
    🔗
    For each step, identify: what theorem or property of \(\R\) does it use, and is completeness needed?
    🔗
    Conclude: In \(\Q\text{,}\) Cauchy sequences need not converge. Give one familiar example.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Step 1: direct argument using the Cauchy property with \(\varepsilon = 1\text{;}\) no completeness needed. Step 2: Bolzano–Weierstrass, which does use completeness (either through MCT or through a nested-intervals argument). Step 3: Triangle-inequality argument; no additional completeness. Thus completeness enters via Step 2. In \(\Q\text{:}\) any Cauchy sequence of rationals converging to \(\sqrt{2}\) in \(\R\text{,}\) e.g., the decimal truncations \(1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, \ldots\text{,}\) is Cauchy in \(\Q\) but has no limit in \(\Q\text{.}\)
