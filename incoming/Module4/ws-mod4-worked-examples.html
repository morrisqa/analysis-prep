    Section 3 Module 4: Worked Examples

    Read each example slowly. The goal is not just to see what the answer is, but to internalize the proof template. Example 1 is the basic \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) pattern you will reproduce on the assessment. Example 2 shows how the Algebra of Limits replaces direct \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) work for rational expressions. Example 3 shows the Monotone Convergence Theorem in its most common use: a recursively defined sequence whose limit is not visible at a glance.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1. Example 1: A Direct \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) Proof.

    Prove directly from the definition that
    \begin{equation*} a_n = \frac{1}{n^2} \longrightarrow 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Scratch work (this section is part of the solution process; always do this before writing the proof).
    🔗
    We want to choose, given \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) an index \(N\) such that
    \begin{equation*} n \geq N \Longrightarrow |a_n - 0| = \frac{1}{n^2} \lt \varepsilon. \end{equation*}
    Solving \(1/n^2 \lt \varepsilon\) for \(n\text{:}\) this happens exactly when \(n^2 \gt 1/\varepsilon\text{,}\) i.e., \(n \gt 1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\text{.}\) So we should choose \(N\) to be any natural number exceeding \(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\text{.}\) The Archimedean Property guarantees such \(N\) exists.
    🔗
    We could also use the weaker but simpler bound \(1/n^2 \leq 1/n\text{,}\) requiring only \(n \gt 1/\varepsilon\text{.}\) Either choice works; we pick the cleaner Archimedean-style bound.
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Let \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{.}\) By the Archimedean Property, there exists \(N \in \N\) with \(N \gt 1/\varepsilon\text{.}\) Then for every \(n \geq N\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} |a_n - 0| = \left|\frac{1}{n^2}\right| = \frac{1}{n^2} \amp\leq \frac{1}{n}\\ \amp\leq \frac{1}{N}\\ \amp\lt \varepsilon, \end{align*}
    where the first inequality uses \(n \geq 1\) (so \(n^2 \geq n\)), the second uses \(n \geq N\text{,}\) and the third uses \(N \gt 1/\varepsilon\text{.}\) Since \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) was arbitrary, \(a_n \to 0\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. The proof has a fixed structure you will reproduce many times: start the formal proof with “Let \(\varepsilon \gt 0\)”, produce \(N\text{,}\) then verify the bound on \(|a_n - L|\) for \(n \geq N\text{.}\) The scratch work lives outside that formal proof but must be shown; the scratch work is what justifies the specific choice of \(N\text{.}\) The chain of inequalities in the proof does the verification. Each step is labeled with a reason: you should do this in your own proofs too.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2. Example 2: A Rational Limit via the Algebra of Limits.

    Evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{3n^2 + 2n - 1}{4n^2 + 5}. \end{equation*}
    Do not use an \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) argument; use the Algebra of Limits (Theorem 2.5) directly.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Set-up. The standard technique for a ratio of polynomials in \(n\) is to divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of \(n\) appearing, which forces every term to converge. Here that is \(n^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Dividing numerator and denominator by \(n^2\text{,}\) we rewrite the general term:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{3n^2 + 2n - 1}{4n^2 + 5} = \frac{3 + \frac{2}{n} - \frac{1}{n^2}}{4 + \frac{5}{n^2}}. \end{equation*}
    We now evaluate the limits of the numerator and denominator separately, using the Algebra of Limits. Throughout, we use the known limit \(1/n \to 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Numerator. By the product rule,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2}{n} = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{n} \longrightarrow 2 \cdot 0 = 0, \qquad \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{1}{n} \cdot \frac{1}{n} \longrightarrow 0 \cdot 0 = 0. \end{equation*}
    The constant sequence 3 has limit 3. Applying the sum rule (twice),
    \begin{equation*} 3 + \frac{2}{n} - \frac{1}{n^2} \longrightarrow 3 + 0 - 0 = 3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Denominator. The constant 4 has limit 4, and \(5/n^2 \to 0\) by the product rule applied to \(5 \cdot (1/n^2)\text{.}\) By the sum rule,
    \begin{equation*} 4 + \frac{5}{n^2} \longrightarrow 4 + 0 = 4. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Quotient. The limit of the denominator is 4, which is nonzero. By the quotient rule,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{3 + \frac{2}{n} - \frac{1}{n^2}}{4 + \frac{5}{n^2}} \longrightarrow \frac{3}{4}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence \(\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{3n^2 + 2n - 1}{4n^2 + 5} = \frac{3}{4}.\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. Once the basic limit \(1/n \to 0\) is established (Example 1 covers \(1/n^2\text{;}\) \(1/n\) is the classical case), every rational expression in \(n\) reduces to a short sequence of limit-law applications. The only subtle point is the nonvanishing denominator hypothesis in the quotient rule: you must confirm in the limit that the denominator is nonzero, which we did here (it equals 4). If the numerator’s leading degree had exceeded the denominator’s, the method would break down (the sequence would diverge to infinity); if the degrees were equal, the limit is always the ratio of leading coefficients.

    Checkpoint 3.3. Example 3: A Recursive Sequence and the Monotone Convergence Theorem.

    Define the sequence \((a_n)\) recursively by
    \begin{equation*} a_1 = 1, \qquad a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n} \quad (n \geq 1). \end{equation*}
    Prove that \((a_n)\) converges and find its limit.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy. The formula for \(a_n\) is not in closed form, so a direct \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) proof is impractical. We instead: (1) show \((a_n)\) is increasing and bounded above, (2) conclude it converges by the Monotone Convergence Theorem, and (3) find the limit by letting \(n \to \infty\) in the recursion.
    🔗
    Step 1: The sequence is bounded above by 2.
    🔗
    We claim \(a_n \lt 2\) for every \(n \in \N\text{,}\) and prove this by induction. Base case. \(a_1 = 1 \lt 2\text{.}\) Inductive step. Suppose \(a_n \lt 2\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n} \lt \sqrt{2 + 2} = \sqrt{4} = 2, \end{equation*}
    using that \(\sqrt{\cdot}\) is monotone increasing on \([0, \infty)\text{.}\) By induction, \(a_n \lt 2\) for all \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: The sequence is increasing.
    🔗
    We claim \(a_n \lt a_{n+1}\) for every \(n \in \N\text{,}\) again by induction. Base case. \(a_1 = 1 \lt \sqrt{3} = a_2\text{.}\) Inductive step. Suppose \(a_n \lt a_{n+1}\text{.}\) Adding 2 and applying the (strictly monotone) square root,
    \begin{equation*} a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n} \lt \sqrt{2 + a_{n+1}} = a_{n+2}. \end{equation*}
    So \(a_{n+1} \lt a_{n+2}\text{.}\) By induction, \((a_n)\) is strictly increasing.
    🔗
    Step 3: Apply the Monotone Convergence Theorem.
    🔗
    By Steps 1 and 2, \((a_n)\) is monotone increasing and bounded above (by 2). By the Monotone Convergence Theorem, \((a_n)\) converges to some limit \(L \in \R\text{.}\) Because all terms satisfy \(1 \leq a_n \lt 2\text{,}\) we have \(L \in [1, 2]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 4: Identify the limit.
    🔗
    Letting \(n \to \infty\) in the recursion \(a_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + a_n}\) and using the continuity of the square root together with the Algebra of Limits (the subsequence \((a_{n+1})\) is just \((a_n)\) shifted by one index, so it shares the limit \(L\)), we get
    \begin{equation*} L = \sqrt{2 + L}. \end{equation*}
    Squaring, \(L^2 = 2 + L\text{,}\) i.e., \(L^2 - L - 2 = 0\text{,}\) which factors as \((L - 2)(L + 1) = 0\text{.}\) So \(L = 2\) or \(L = -1\text{.}\) Since \(L \in [1, 2]\text{,}\) we conclude \(L = 2\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. This is the archetypal pattern for a recursively defined sequence. Three moves, always in this order: (1) prove boundedness by induction, often guessing the bound by first solving the fixed-point equation informally; (2) prove monotonicity by induction, often driven by a monotone function applied to the recursion; (3) apply MCT to get a limit, then solve a fixed-point equation for its value. Note how the Completeness Axiom enters through MCT: without completeness, Step 3 could fail (the sequence would be Cauchy in \(\R\) but might lack a limit in a smaller ordered field). The continuity of the square root used in Step 4 is a fact we will prove formally in Module 6; here we use it informally, as Zorn does in §2.3.
