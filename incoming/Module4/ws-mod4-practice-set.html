    Section 4 Module 4: Practice Problem Set

    Problems are labeled \([\mathbf{F}]\) (Foundational), \([\mathbf{S}]\) (Standard), and \([\mathbf{C}]\) (Challenge). Work in order within each level; later problems in a level often build on earlier ones.
    🔗
    These problems are not submitted, but you should write out complete solutions. For any problem that asks you to prove something, apply the standards from the Worked Examples: scratch work labeled and separate from the formal proof, complete sentences, every step justified.
    🔗

    Foundational Problems.

    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.1. \([\mathbf{F}]\) Limit Intuition.

    For each sequence, state (without proof) its limit, or state that it diverges. A one-sentence reason is enough.
    1. \(\displaystyle a_n = \dfrac{3}{n + 5}\)
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle a_n = \dfrac{2n + 1}{n}\)
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle a_n = (-1)^n + \dfrac{1}{n}\)
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle a_n = \dfrac{n^2}{n + 1}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) Converges to 0 (numerator fixed, denominator \(\to \infty\)). (b) \(2 + 1/n \to 2\text{.}\) (c) Diverges: even subsequence \(\to 1\text{,}\) odd subsequence \(\to -1\text{.}\) (d) \(a_n = n - n/(n+1)\text{,}\) which grows without bound; diverges.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.2. \([\mathbf{F}]\) Finding \(N\) Given \(\varepsilon\).

    For \(a_n = 1/n\) and each of the given tolerances \(\varepsilon\text{,}\) find the smallest \(N \in \N\) such that \(|a_n - 0| \lt \varepsilon\) for all \(n \geq N\text{.}\) Show your work.
    1. \(\displaystyle \varepsilon = 0.1\)
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \varepsilon = 0.01\)
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \varepsilon = 1/10^6\)
      🔗
    4. (general) \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) in terms of \(\varepsilon\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    We need \(n \gt 1/\varepsilon\text{.}\) (a) \(N = 11\) (smallest integer exceeding 10). (b) \(N = 101\text{.}\) (c) \(N = 10^6 + 1\text{.}\) (d) Any integer \(N \gt 1/\varepsilon\text{;}\) by the Archimedean Property, such \(N\) exists, and one standard choice is \(N = \lfloor 1/\varepsilon \rfloor + 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.3. \([\mathbf{F}]\) Bounded, Monotone, or Both?

    For each sequence, decide whether it is (i) bounded, (ii) monotone (increasing or decreasing), or (iii) neither. Briefly justify each answer.
    1. \(\displaystyle a_n = n\)
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle a_n = (-1)^n\)
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle a_n = \dfrac{n}{n + 1}\)
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle a_n = \dfrac{1}{n^2}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) Monotone increasing; not bounded (above). (b) Bounded; not monotone. (c) Bounded (\(0 \lt a_n \lt 1\)) and monotone increasing (check \(a_{n+1} - a_n = 1/((n+1)(n+2)) \gt 0\)). (d) Bounded (\(0 \lt a_n \leq 1\)) and monotone decreasing.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.4. \([\mathbf{F}]\) Algebra of Limits.

    Use the Algebra of Limits (and the known limit \(1/n \to 0\)) to evaluate each limit. Write out which limit law you invoke at each step.
    1. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(5 - \dfrac{3}{n}\right)\)
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{2n - 1}{n + 4}\)
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \dfrac{n^2 + 3}{2n^2 - n}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) \(5 - 0 = 5\) (sum/difference rule). (b) Divide by \(n\text{:}\) \((2 - 1/n)/(1 + 4/n) \to 2/1 = 2\) (sum, quotient, \(1/n \to 0\)). (c) Divide by \(n^2\text{:}\) \((1 + 3/n^2)/(2 - 1/n) \to 1/2\) (sum, quotient, \(1/n^2 \to 0\) via product rule, \(1/n \to 0\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Standard Problems.

    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.5. \([\mathbf{S}]\) A Direct \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) Proof.

    Prove directly from the definition of convergence that
    \begin{equation*} a_n = \frac{5n + 3}{2n + 1} \longrightarrow \frac{5}{2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Your proof must follow the template from Worked Example 1: explicit scratch work, clearly labeled, that derives the relationship between \(n\) and \(\varepsilon\text{;}\) followed by the formal proof written in complete sentences, with every inequality in the verification step justified.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Compute
    \begin{equation*} \left|\frac{5n + 3}{2n + 1} - \frac{5}{2}\right| = \left|\frac{2(5n+3) - 5(2n+1)}{2(2n+1)}\right| = \frac{1}{2(2n+1)}. \end{equation*}
    Then find the condition on \(n\) for this to be less than \(\varepsilon\text{,}\) and apply the Archimedean Property.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.6. \([\mathbf{S}]\) The Squeeze Theorem.

    Prove that
    \begin{equation*} b_n = \frac{\sin(n)}{n} \longrightarrow 0. \end{equation*}
    Use the Squeeze Theorem (Theorem 2.6). Your proof should (i) explicitly identify two bounding sequences \(a_n \leq b_n \leq c_n\text{,}\) (ii) state what each of those sequences converges to (and why), and (iii) invoke the Squeeze Theorem by name.
    🔗
    Hint.
    For any real number \(t\text{,}\) \(-1 \leq \sin(t) \leq 1\text{,}\) so \(-1/n \leq \sin(n)/n \leq 1/n\text{.}\) Both outer sequences tend to 0.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.7. \([\mathbf{S}]\) Convergent Implies Bounded.

    Prove that if \(a_n \to L\text{,}\) then \((a_n)\) is bounded. Write a complete proof. (This is Theorem 2.4 in Zorn; the argument was sketched in the study guide.)
    🔗
    Your proof should use the definition of convergence with \(\varepsilon = 1\) to bound the tail of the sequence, then use the finiteness of the initial segment to bound the whole sequence.
    🔗
    Hint.
    By convergence with \(\varepsilon = 1\text{,}\) pick \(N\) so that \(|a_n - L| \lt 1\) for \(n \geq N\text{.}\) The Triangle Inequality then gives \(|a_n| \leq |L| + 1\) for \(n \geq N\text{.}\) Bound \(|a_1|, \ldots, |a_{N-1}|\) by their maximum, and combine.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.8. \([\mathbf{S}]\) Proving Divergence via Negation.

    Prove that the sequence \(a_n = (-1)^n\) diverges.
    🔗
    Your argument must use the negation of the definition of convergence (as developed in the study guide): for any candidate limit \(L \in \R\text{,}\) exhibit a specific \(\varepsilon_0 \gt 0\) for which the convergence definition fails.
    🔗
    A proof via two subsequences with different limits is elegant but not what this problem asks for. Use the raw definition.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Let \(L \in \R\text{.}\) Since adjacent terms satisfy \(|a_n - a_{n+1}| = 2\text{,}\) the Triangle Inequality implies \(|a_n - L| + |a_{n+1} - L| \geq 2\text{,}\) so at least one of the two is at least 1. Use this with \(\varepsilon_0 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Challenge Problems.

    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.9. \([\mathbf{C}]\) A Cauchy Proof from Definition.

    Let \(a_n = \dfrac{1}{n}\text{.}\) Prove directly from the definition of Cauchy sequence that \((a_n)\) is Cauchy.
    🔗
    You may not use the theorem “convergent implies Cauchy” even though it would close this problem in one line. The goal is to produce, given \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) an explicit \(N \in \N\) such that \(m, n \geq N \Rightarrow |a_m - a_n| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Without loss of generality \(m \geq n \geq N\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} |a_m - a_n| = \left|\frac{1}{m} - \frac{1}{n}\right| = \frac{n - m}{mn} \cdot (\text{sign}) \end{equation*}
    so \(|a_m - a_n| \leq 1/n \leq 1/N\text{.}\) Choose \(N \gt 1/\varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.10. \([\mathbf{C}]\) Applying Bolzano–Weierstrass.

    Let \((a_n)\) be a bounded sequence of real numbers. Suppose that every convergent subsequence of \((a_n)\) converges to the same limit \(L\text{.}\) Prove that \(a_n \to L\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This is the converse direction of the subsequence-inheritance result, with an extra hypothesis. It illustrates why Bolzano–Weierstrass is the right tool for controlling sequences whose convergence you cannot verify directly.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Proof by contradiction. If \(a_n \not\to L\text{,}\) there exists \(\varepsilon_0 \gt 0\) and a subsequence \((a_{n_k})\) with \(|a_{n_k} - L| \geq \varepsilon_0\) for all \(k\text{.}\) This subsequence is bounded; by Bolzano–Weierstrass it has a further convergent sub-subsequence, which by hypothesis converges to \(L\text{.}\) But all its terms are at least \(\varepsilon_0\) away from \(L\) — contradiction.
