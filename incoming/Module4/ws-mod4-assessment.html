    Section 6 Module 4: Assessment

    This assessment has three parts. Parts 1 and 2 ask you to write complete mathematical proofs, submitted for feedback. Part 3 is a short reflection. Submit all three parts together as a single document (PDF or typed file) through the course management system.
    🔗
    Write-up standard. All proofs must be written in complete mathematical prose. Use the style modeled in the Worked Examples: include scratch work (clearly labeled) before the formal proof, announce your strategy, write in complete grammatical sentences, and justify every step. Work consisting only of symbolic manipulations without explanation will not receive full credit.
    🔗
    A note on Part 2. All students complete all three parts. For Part 2, choose the option that best matches your background: if you have prior real analysis experience, challenge yourself with Option B. If this is newer territory, Option A is the right starting point.
    🔗
    Academic integrity. Your proofs must be your own work. You may refer to your notes, the textbooks, and the worked examples from this module. You may not collaborate with other students on the written proofs or use AI writing tools to draft your arguments.
    🔗
    Part 1: Core Proof (Required) (Estimated time: 25 minutes)
    🔗
    Consider the sequence defined by
    \begin{equation*} a_n = \frac{4n + 7}{2n + 5}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.1. Conjecturing the Limit.

    Based on the Algebra of Limits (or on straightforward algebra on the terms), identify the value of \(\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Show your algebra clearly. This work does not need to be in formal proof style; it is the analysis step that precedes the \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) proof.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Divide numerator and denominator by \(n\text{:}\) \(a_n = (4 + 7/n)/(2 + 5/n)\text{.}\) Each of \(7/n\) and \(5/n\) tends to 0.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2. An \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) Proof of the Limit.

    Using your work in the previous problem, prove directly from the \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) definition that
    \begin{equation*} a_n = \frac{4n + 7}{2n + 5} \longrightarrow 2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Your proof must have the following structure:
    1. Scratch work (clearly labeled). Simplify \(|a_n - 2|\) by combining over a common denominator. Determine the condition on \(n\) under which \(|a_n - 2| \lt \varepsilon\text{,}\) and describe how you will choose \(N\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Formal proof. Write a complete proof beginning “Let \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{.}\)” Produce \(N \in \N\) (invoking the Archimedean Property by name), and verify that \(n \geq N\) implies \(|a_n - 2| \lt \varepsilon\text{,}\) with every inequality in the verification labeled with its justification.
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    A direct calculation gives
    \begin{equation*} |a_n - 2| = \left|\frac{4n + 7 - 2(2n + 5)}{2n + 5}\right| = \frac{3}{2n + 5}. \end{equation*}
    Requiring \(3/(2n + 5) \lt \varepsilon\) is equivalent to \(n \gt (3/\varepsilon - 5)/2\text{.}\) A slightly cleaner choice: since \(2n + 5 \gt n\) for all \(n \in \N\text{,}\) we have \(|a_n - 2| \lt 3/n\text{,}\) so it is enough to require \(n \gt 3/\varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.3. The Role of Completeness.

    In one or two sentences, explain where the Completeness Axiom enters your proof in the previous problem. (Hint: does it enter directly, or through the Archimedean Property?)
    🔗
    🔗
    Part 2: Proof Choice (Required — Choose One) (Estimated time: 15 minutes)
    🔗
    Choose one of the following two proof problems. Both ask for a complete proof; they differ in difficulty and the techniques required. Label your submission clearly with “Option A” or “Option B.”
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.4. Option A (Standard): A Limit via the Algebra of Limits.

    Evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{3n^2 - n}{n^2 + 4}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Write a complete solution using the Algebra of Limits (Theorem 2.5) and the known limit \(1/n \to 0\text{.}\) Do not write an \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) proof.
    🔗
    Your solution must: (i) begin with an algebraic rewrite of the expression that puts every term in a form whose limit you know, (ii) evaluate the numerator and denominator limits separately, stating which limit law you invoke at each step, and (iii) verify the nonvanishing hypothesis of the quotient rule before applying it.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Divide numerator and denominator by \(n^2\text{.}\) The limit law on quotients requires the denominator’s limit to be nonzero; confirm this before taking the quotient.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.5. Option B (More Challenging): Convergent Implies Cauchy.

    Prove directly from the definitions that every convergent sequence of real numbers is Cauchy.
    🔗
    That is: suppose \((a_n)\) is a sequence with \(a_n \to L\text{.}\) Prove that for every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) there exists \(N \in \N\) such that \(m, n \geq N\) implies \(|a_m - a_n| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Your proof must clearly identify: what you assume from the convergence hypothesis, how you split \(\varepsilon\text{,}\) and where the Triangle Inequality is applied. Your proof should not use any theorem beyond the \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) definition and the Triangle Inequality.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Apply the convergence definition with tolerance \(\varepsilon/2\) to obtain \(N \in \N\) with \(|a_k - L| \lt \varepsilon/2\) for \(k \geq N\text{.}\) Then for \(m, n \geq N\text{,}\) write \(a_m - a_n = (a_m - L) + (L - a_n)\) and apply the Triangle Inequality.
    🔗

    Part 3: Reflection (Estimated time: 5 minutes)
    🔗
    Write 3–5 sentences for each question. These are graded for thoughtfulness, not for mathematical correctness.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.6. Quantifier Alternation.

    In your Part 1(b) proof, where in the proof did you make a choice that depended on \(\varepsilon\text{?}\) What would have broken in the proof if your choice of \(N\) had been made before seeing \(\varepsilon\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.7. Bauldry’s Presentation.

    In the Bridge Reading you read Bauldry’s treatment of sequences (§2.5). Compare the presentation style to Zorn’s: what is more compressed in Bauldry, and what feels more like the Zorn text you worked through in the study guide? If there is a single Bauldry sentence that you had to read more than once to understand, quote it and describe what was implicit.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.8. Looking Ahead.

    In Module 6, you will prove that a function \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) using an \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) argument: for every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) there exists \(\delta \gt 0\) such that \(|x - a| \lt \delta\) implies \(|f(x) - f(a)| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\) Based on what you practiced in this module, describe the connection you see between the \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) structure of a sequence convergence proof and the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) structure of a continuity proof. What is similar? What role does \(N\) play that \(\delta\) will play?
