    Section 3 Module 1: Worked Examples

    This document contains five carefully annotated worked examples. Each proof is written in the style expected in MAT 5610: complete mathematical prose, clearly organized, with every step justified. Read each proof twice: first for the mathematics, then for the structure. The annotations in brackets explain why each step is taken.
    🔗
    How to use these examples. After reading each proof, cover the solution and try to reconstruct it from memory. Then compare your reconstruction to the original. Pay particular attention to the opening sentence of each proof, which announces the strategy.
    🔗
    Example 1: Quantifier Translation and Negation
    🔗
    Problem. Let the universe be \(\R\text{.}\)
    1. Translate into symbols: “For every positive real number \(\varepsilon\text{,}\) there exists a natural number \(N\) such that \(1/N \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\)”
      🔗
    2. Write the negation of your symbolic statement.
      🔗
    3. Translate the negation back into plain English.
      🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1. Try It: Translate and Negate.

    Before reading the solution, write your own symbolic translation and negation for the problem above.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Part (a): Symbolic translation.
    🔗
    [Identify each piece before writing the formula. The quantifiers appear in order: first \(\varepsilon\) is universally quantified, then \(N\) is existentially quantified.]
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \forall \varepsilon \gt 0 \;\; \exists N \in \N \text{ such that } \frac{1}{N} \lt \varepsilon. \end{equation*}
    [Note: \(\forall \varepsilon \gt 0\) is shorthand for \(\forall \varepsilon \in \R\) with \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{.}\) This is standard in analysis. We could also write \(\forall \varepsilon \in (0, \infty)\text{.}\)]
    🔗
    Part (b): Negation.
    🔗
    [To negate, flip every quantifier and negate the innermost predicate. The rule is: \(\neg \forall = \exists\) and \(\neg \exists = \forall\text{.}\) We also flip the inequality.]
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \exists \varepsilon \gt 0 \text{ such that } \forall N \in \N, \;\; \frac{1}{N} \geq \varepsilon. \end{equation*}
    [Check: every \(\forall\) became \(\exists\text{,}\) every \(\exists\) became \(\forall\text{,}\) and \(\lt\) became \(\geq\text{.}\) The structure is completely parallel but reversed.]
    🔗
    Part (c): English translation of the negation.
    🔗
    “There exists a positive real number \(\varepsilon\) such that \(1/N \geq \varepsilon\) for every natural number \(N\text{.}\)”
    🔗
    [Interpretation: the negation says that \(1/N\) does not approach 0. This is false, since the original statement is true (Archimedean property). But the negation is a valid, well-formed mathematical claim — it just happens to be false.]
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Example 2: Direct Proof (via Contradiction)
    🔗
    Claim. If \(a\) is rational and \(b\) is irrational, then \(a + b\) is irrational.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2. Try It: Rational Plus Irrational.

    Before reading the solution, attempt a proof of the claim above. Which strategy will you use?
    🔗
    Solution.
    [Strategy choice: We want to show \(a + b\) is irrational. Direct proof seems hard here — irrationality means “not rational,” which is negative information. Proof by contradiction is a natural fit.]
    🔗

    Proof.
    Assume, toward contradiction, that \(a + b\) is rational.
    🔗
    [Contradiction setup: we assume the opposite of what we want to prove. We now have two assumptions: \(a\) is rational, and \(a + b\) is rational. We expect to derive a contradiction of the assumption that \(b\) is irrational.]
    🔗
    Since \(a\) is rational and \(a + b\) is rational (by assumption), and since the rationals are closed under subtraction, the quantity
    \begin{equation*} (a + b) - a = b \end{equation*}
    is rational.
    🔗
    [Key step: we used closure of \(\Q\) under subtraction. This is analogous to closure under addition — both require writing out the definitions and checking denominators. Here we cite it as a known fact.]
    🔗
    But this contradicts the hypothesis that \(b\) is irrational. Therefore, our assumption was false, and \(a + b\) must be irrational. \(\square\)
    🔗
    [The contradiction closes the proof. Notice the final sentence explicitly states what was shown and how.]
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3: Proof by Contrapositive
    🔗
    Claim. For integers \(m\) and \(n\text{,}\) if \(mn\) is odd, then both \(m\) and \(n\) are odd.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3. Try It: Odd Product.

    Before reading the solution, identify the contrapositive of the claim. Which logical law do you need to form the negation of “\(m\) is odd and \(n\) is odd”?
    🔗
    Solution.
    [Identify the structure: \(P\) = “\(mn\) is odd”; \(Q\) = “\(m\) is odd and \(n\) is odd”. The contrapositive is: if \(m\) is even or \(n\) is even, then \(mn\) is even. Note: \(\neg(A \wedge B) \equiv \neg A \vee \neg B\) by De Morgan’s law.]
    🔗

    Proof.
    We prove the contrapositive: if \(m\) or \(n\) is even, then \(mn\) is even.
    🔗
    [The opening sentence announces the strategy and states the contrapositive clearly. This is good mathematical prose: the reader knows immediately what to expect.]
    🔗
    Without loss of generality, suppose \(m\) is even.
    🔗
    [“Without loss of generality” (WLOG) is justified here because the argument for \(n\) even is identical by symmetry. Only use WLOG when the cases truly are symmetric.]
    🔗
    Then \(m = 2k\) for some integer \(k\text{.}\) Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} mn = (2k)n = 2(kn), \end{equation*}
    which is even, since \(kn\) is an integer.
    🔗
    [The computation is brief but complete. We use the definition of “even” (divisible by 2) in both the hypothesis and the conclusion.]
    🔗
    By the contrapositive, if \(mn\) is odd, then both \(m\) and \(n\) are odd. \(\square\)
    🔗
    [The final sentence explicitly invokes the contrapositive to close the loop. Do not leave the reader to infer this step.]
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4: Proof by Mathematical Induction
    🔗
    Claim. For all integers \(n \geq 1\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=1}^{n} (2k - 1) = n^2. \end{equation*}
    (The left side is the sum of the first \(n\) odd numbers.)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4. Try It: Sum of Odd Numbers.

    Before reading the solution, verify the formula for \(n = 1, 2, 3\text{.}\) Then attempt the induction proof, being sure to label the base case, induction hypothesis, and inductive step.
    🔗
    Solution.
    [Verify a few cases first: \(n = 1\text{:}\) LHS = 1, RHS = 1. \(n = 2\text{:}\) LHS = \(1 + 3 = 4\text{,}\) RHS = 4. \(n = 3\text{:}\) LHS = \(1 + 3 + 5 = 9\text{,}\) RHS = 9. Good. Now write the formal proof.]
    🔗

    Proof.
    We prove the claim by mathematical induction. Let \(P(n)\) denote the statement \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n (2k-1) = n^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    [Name your predicate. This makes the base case and inductive step easier to state precisely.]
    🔗
    Base case \((n = 1)\text{:}\) The left side is \(\sum_{k=1}^1 (2k-1) = 2(1) - 1 = 1\text{.}\) The right side is \(1^2 = 1\text{.}\) Since they are equal, \(P(1)\) holds.
    🔗
    [The base case is verified explicitly, not just asserted. For simple cases one line suffices.]
    🔗
    Inductive step. Assume \(P(n)\) holds for some integer \(n \geq 1\text{;}\) that is, assume
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=1}^{n} (2k-1) = n^2. \quad\text{(Induction Hypothesis)} \end{equation*}
    We must show \(P(n+1)\text{:}\) that \(\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}(2k-1) = (n+1)^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    [State the induction hypothesis explicitly and label it. Then state what you must prove. This structure is non-negotiable in a correct induction proof.]
    🔗
    Computing:
    \begin{align*} \sum_{k=1}^{n+1}(2k-1) \;&=\; \left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(2k-1)\right) + (2(n+1) - 1)\\ \;&=\; n^2 + (2n + 1) \quad\text{(Induction Hypothesis)}\\ \;&=\; (n+1)^2. \end{align*}
    🔗
    [The second line cites the induction hypothesis. Always label where you use it — this is the heart of the argument.]
    🔗
    This is exactly \(P(n+1)\text{,}\) so the inductive step is complete.
    🔗
    Conclusion. By the principle of mathematical induction, \(P(n)\) holds for all integers \(n \geq 1\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    [The conclusion explicitly invokes the induction principle. Do not end an induction proof without this closing statement.]
    🔗

    Example 5: An Iff Proof (Two Directions)
    🔗
    Claim. An integer \(n\) is even if and only if \(n^2\) is even.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.5. Try It: Even Iff Square Is Even.

    An iff proof requires two separate arguments. Before reading, identify which strategy you expect to use for each direction, and why the two directions may require different approaches.
    🔗
    Solution.
    [An “iff” proof requires proving both directions: \(n \text{ even} \Rightarrow n^2 \text{ even}\) and \(n^2 \text{ even} \Rightarrow n \text{ even}\text{.}\) These may require different strategies.]
    🔗

    Proof.
    \((\Rightarrow)\) Assume \(n\) is even. We show \(n^2\) is even.
    🔗
    [This direction is direct: we have concrete information about \(n\text{.}\)]
    🔗
    Since \(n\) is even, write \(n = 2m\) for some integer \(m\text{.}\) Then \(n^2 = (2m)^2 = 4m^2 = 2(2m^2)\text{,}\) which is even.
    🔗
    \((\Leftarrow)\) Assume \(n^2\) is even. We show \(n\) is even.
    🔗
    [This direction has an awkward hypothesis. We know \(n^2 = 2j\) for some integer \(j\text{,}\) but it is not obvious how to get from there to a statement about \(n\text{.}\) The contrapositive is cleaner: if \(n\) is odd, then \(n^2\) is odd.]
    🔗
    We prove the contrapositive: if \(n\) is odd, then \(n^2\) is odd. Suppose \(n\) is odd, so \(n = 2m + 1\) for some integer \(m\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} n^2 = (2m+1)^2 = 4m^2 + 4m + 1 = 2(2m^2 + 2m) + 1, \end{equation*}
    which is odd.
    🔗
    [The two directions used different strategies: direct for \((\Rightarrow)\) and contrapositive for \((\Leftarrow)\text{.}\) This is entirely normal and often necessary. Do not force both directions into the same strategy.]
    🔗
    Since both directions hold, \(n\) is even if and only if \(n^2\) is even. \(\square\)
