    Section 5 Module 1: Bridge Reading Guide

    This guide bridges Module 1 to the text you will be reading on day one of MAT 5610. The assignment is to read the opening six pages of Bauldry Chapter 2 (“Introduction to Real Analysis”), specifically pages 45–50, which cover the chapter introduction and §2.1 (Basic Topology of the Real Numbers) through the end of Theorem 2.7.
    🔗
    Your goal is not to master the topology. That material will be developed carefully in Modules 2 and 3. Your goal here is narrower and more focused: to see how the logical language you practiced in Module 1 — quantifiers, negation, implication, proof strategies — appears immediately and everywhere in Bauldry’s text. Every definition and theorem in these pages is a quantified statement. Every proof deploys one of the strategies you have just practiced.
    🔗
    How to read. Read with a pencil. Before answering each question below, mark up the Bauldry page: box the quantifiers, underline hypotheses, circle conclusions. This active reading habit is essential in MAT 5610.
    🔗
    A note on approach. If this material feels familiar from a prior real analysis course, use this reading to practice working at full speed with pencil in hand, focusing especially on the logical structure questions rather than the content. If this is your first encounter with analysis, read slowly and work every question in order. It is normal for some of the topology vocabulary (open, closed, accumulation point) to be unfamiliar — flag those terms and continue. The questions are designed so that you can answer them using the tools from Module 1 alone.
    🔗
    Part 1: The Chapter Introduction (p. 45) (Suggested time: 5 minutes)
    🔗
    Read the chapter introduction on page 45 (the paragraph beginning “We begin our study of real analysis…”).
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1. Logical Development vs. Calculation.

    Bauldry describes Chapter 2 as covering “essentially the same topics as Chapter 1… but now from a perspective based on logical development and proof.” Based on what you know from the Module 1 orientation, what does it mean for a mathematical development to be based on logical development and proof rather than just on calculation? What specific tools from Module 1 make this shift possible?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.2. Think Deeply of Simple Things.

    Bauldry quotes Arnold Ross: “think deeply of simple things.” How does this motto connect to the proof-writing advice in Zorn §1.4 (your Study Guide Reading 1)? Identify one specific rule from Zorn that exemplifies this motto.
    🔗
    🔗
    Part 2: Reading Definitions as Quantified Statements (pp. 46–49) (Suggested time: 15 minutes)
    🔗
    The heart of this bridge reading is learning to parse Bauldry’s definitions using the quantifier machinery from Module 1. Work through the questions below as you read the definitions on pp. 46–49.
    🔗
    Definition 2.1: Least Upper Bound Property (p. 47). Bauldry writes: “\(U\) has the least upper bound property if, whenever a nonempty subset \(E \subseteq U\) is bounded above, then \(E\) has a least upper bound in \(U\text{.}\)” This is an implication embedded inside a universal claim.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3. LUB Property in Quantifier Notation.

    Rewrite the Least Upper Bound Property in explicit quantifier notation. Use the following setup: let the universe be an ordered set \(U\text{,}\) let \(E\) range over nonempty subsets of \(U\text{,}\) and let “\(E\) is bounded above” and “\(E\) has a least upper bound in \(U\)” be predicates. Your symbolic statement should have the form \(\forall E \ldots \bigl( P(E) \Rightarrow Q(E) \bigr)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.4. Negating the LUB Property.

    Write the negation of the Least Upper Bound Property. In plain English, what would it mean for a set \(U\) to fail to have the least upper bound property? (Bauldry says \(\Q\) fails to have this property. Can you give a specific example?)
    🔗
    🔗
    Theorem 2.3: Archimedean Order Property (p. 48). Theorem 2.3 states: If \(\varepsilon\) and \(x\) are positive real numbers, then there is an \(n \in \N\) such that \(n\varepsilon \gt x\text{.}\) Compare Corollary 2.4, Item 3: For any positive real number \(x\text{,}\) there is an \(n \in \N\) such that \(1/n \lt x\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.5. Archimedean Property in Quantifier Notation.

    Write Theorem 2.3 in full quantifier notation, identifying: (a) the universal quantifiers and their domains; (b) the existential quantifier and its domain; (c) the predicate that must hold. Then compare your formula to the statement you translated in Worked Example 1 (Archimedean property). Are they the same statement?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.6. Reading Bauldry’s Proof of Theorem 2.3.

    Read Bauldry’s proof of Theorem 2.3 carefully.
    1. What proof strategy does Bauldry use? (Direct, contrapositive, or contradiction?) How do you know?
      🔗
    2. Identify the sentence where Bauldry assumes the opposite of the conclusion. Write it out.
      🔗
    3. What is the contradiction Bauldry derives, and which theorem does he invoke to derive it?
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.7. Corollary 2.4, Item 3: Negation and Structure.

    Corollary 2.4 lists four equivalent forms of the Archimedean property. For Item 3 (\(\forall x \gt 0 \; \exists n \in \N\) s.t. \(1/n \lt x\)):
    1. Write the negation in symbols and in plain English.
      🔗
    2. Compare the negation to your answer in Practice Problem 1-3(c) (negation of the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) statement). What structural similarity do you notice?
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Definitions 2.2 and 2.3: Metric Spaces and Neighborhoods (pp. 48–49). Bauldry defines a metric space as a set \(X\) with a distance function \(d : X \to \R\) satisfying three properties, and then defines a neighborhood of a point \(x\) as \(N_r(x) = \{y \mid d(x, y) \lt r\}\) for some \(r \gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.8. Metric Properties in Quantifier Notation.

    The three properties of a metric are:
    1. \(d(x,y) = 0\) if and only if \(x = y\)
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle d(x,y) = d(y,x)\)
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle d(x,z) \leq d(x,y) + d(y,z)\)
      🔗
    Each of these is implicitly universally quantified over all \(x, y, z \in X\text{.}\) Write property 1 out in full quantifier notation, including the “if and only if” structure. (You will need two implications; recall how iff proofs work from Module 1.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.9. Accumulation Points.

    Definition 2.3 defines a point \(x\) to be an accumulation point (or limit point) of a set \(E\) if every deleted neighborhood of \(x\) contains a point of \(E\) different from \(x\text{;}\) that is,
    \begin{equation*} \forall r \gt 0, \; N_r'(x) \cap E \neq \emptyset. \end{equation*}
    1. Write the negation of this statement. What would it mean for \(x\) to not be an accumulation point of \(E\text{?}\)
      🔗
    2. Bauldry calls a point that is not an accumulation point an isolated point. Does your negation match Definition 2.3 item 8?
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Part 3: Reading a Graduate-Level Proof (pp. 47–50) (Suggested time: 10 minutes)
    🔗
    In MAT 5610 you will be expected to read proofs like those in Bauldry and identify their logical structure. This section practices exactly that.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.10. Theorem 2.2: Infimum Theorem.

    Read the proof of Theorem 2.2 on page 47 (the infimum theorem: if \(B \subseteq U\) is bounded below in a set with the LUB property, then \(\inf B\) exists and is in \(U\)).
    1. State the hypothesis and conclusion of Theorem 2.2 as an implication in the form \(P \Rightarrow Q\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Bauldry’s proof constructs a new set \(L\text{.}\) What is \(L\text{,}\) and what role does the LUB property play in the proof?
      🔗
    3. Is this a direct proof, a proof by contrapositive, or a proof by contradiction? Justify your answer.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.11. Theorem 2.7: Open Sets Under Union.

    Theorem 2.7 (p. 50) states four results about unions and intersections of open and closed sets. Consider part 1: “For any collection of open sets \(\{O_\alpha\}\text{,}\) the union \(\bigcup_\alpha O_\alpha\) is open.”
    1. Write this statement using an explicit universal quantifier over points \(x\) in the union. (Hint: a set is open iff every point is an interior point, i.e., has a neighborhood contained in the set.)
      🔗
    2. Trace through Bauldry’s one-paragraph proof. Identify the moment the proof uses the definition of “open” and the moment it uses the definition of “union.”
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.12. Completeness as an Iff.

    Bauldry ends page 50 by noting that a metric space is complete “if and only if every Cauchy sequence converges.”
    1. This is an iff definition. Write it with the biconditional arrow \(\Leftrightarrow\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Based on your Module 1 work: to verify that a metric space is complete, which direction of the iff would you need to prove? To show it is not complete, what would you need to exhibit?
      🔗
    🔗

    Part 4: Synthesis Reflection (pp. 45–50) (Suggested time: 5 minutes)
    🔗
    After completing the guided questions, take a few minutes for the following reflection. Write two to four sentences for each. These reflections are not graded but will directly prepare you for the Module 1 assessment.

    Checkpoint 5.13. Quantifiers Everywhere.

    Look back at the six pages you just read. Count how many times the symbols \(\forall\) or \(\exists\) appear explicitly, or are implied by phrases like “for every,” “there exists,” “for any,” “whenever,” or “for some.” What does this count tell you about the role of quantifiers in graduate-level mathematics?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.14. Flag an Unfamiliar Term.

    Identify one definition or theorem from pp. 45–50 that you could not fully parse after reading Module 1 alone. What specific term or concept is unfamiliar? (This is a preview of Modules 2 and 3; flagging it now will help you engage with those modules more deliberately.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.15. Contradiction and Existence Claims.

    Consider the following sentence from Bauldry’s proof of Theorem 2.3: “Suppose no such \(n\) exists.”
    1. What proof strategy does this sentence signal?
      🔗
    2. Write the formal negation of the conclusion of Theorem 2.3, i.e., the statement that Bauldry is temporarily assuming.
      🔗
    3. Why is this strategy well-suited to an existence claim?
