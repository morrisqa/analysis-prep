    Section 1 Module 1: Mathematical Language and Proof Strategies

    Objectives

    • Correctly read and write mathematical statements using \(\forall\) and \(\exists\text{,}\) including statements with multiple quantifiers.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Form the negation of a quantified statement, applying De Morgan’s laws and quantifier-negation rules accurately.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Identify the hypothesis and conclusion of an implication, and correctly state its converse and contrapositive.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Choose and execute an appropriate proof strategy (direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction, or induction) for a given claim.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Write proofs that are clear, complete, and in grammatically correct mathematical prose.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Read a short passage from Bauldry and identify the logical structure of its definitions and theorems.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Welcome to the first module of Intensive Analysis Prep. Whether you are refreshing skills from a real analysis course taken years ago, or building on a proof-based course from a different area of mathematics, this module starts in the same place for everyone: the language mathematicians use to make precise statements, and the strategies they use to prove them.
    🔗
    The self-assessment questions below are not graded. They are designed to help you locate yourself within the module and flag concepts that may need extra attention. Write informally; a sentence or two per question is fine.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.1. Quantifiers.

    Consider the two statements:
    1. \(\displaystyle \forall x \in \R \;\; \exists n \in \N \text{ such that } x \lt n\)
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \exists n \in \N \text{ such that } \forall x \in \R,\;\; x \lt n\)
      🔗
    In plain English, what does each statement say? Which is true and which is false?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.2. Negation.

    Write the negation of: “For every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) there exists \(\delta \gt 0\) such that \(|x - a| \lt \delta\) implies \(|f(x) - L| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\)”
    🔗
    (This is the definition of \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\text{.}\) Its negation is the statement that the limit does not equal \(L\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.3. Proof Strategies.

    For each claim, identify whether you would first try direct proof, proof by contrapositive, or proof by contradiction—and say briefly why.
    1. If \(n^2\) is odd, then \(n\) is odd.
      🔗
    2. There are infinitely many prime numbers.
      🔗
    3. If \(a\) and \(b\) are rational, then \(a + b\) is rational.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4. Proof by Induction.

    Recall that a proof by induction on \(n\) requires two steps. Name them and state briefly what each requires you to show.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5. Your Background.

    Briefly describe your proof-writing background. Have you taken a formal proofs course (like one using Hirst)? A real analysis course? Neither? How long ago? What felt most comfortable, and what felt hardest?

    🔗

    A Note from the Instructor.

    Graduate real analysis is hard because it demands two things simultaneously: mathematical content (what is true) and logical precision (why it must be true, expressed unambiguously). Most students find the content familiar—you have seen sequences, limits, and derivatives before. The challenge in MAT 5610 is the second part: Bauldry’s definitions are short but dense, and every word is load-bearing.
    🔗
    This module builds the foundation for that precision. The quantifiers \(\forall\) and \(\exists\) will appear in virtually every definition we encounter this summer. The proof strategies covered here are the exact strategies Bauldry’s proofs rely on, often without naming them. If you breeze through the self-assessment above, spend your time on the Bridge Reading and the harder practice problems. If the self-assessment reveals gaps, work through all the materials carefully. Either way, do not skip the Worked Examples.
    🔗
    🔗

    A Note on Pacing.

    A note on pacing. This module covers material that is foundational for everything that follows, so invest time here even if the topics feel familiar. If you have prior real analysis experience: you may move quickly through the Study Guide and Worked Examples, but spend extra time on the Bridge Reading—the goal is not just to recall proof strategies but to see how Bauldry deploys them in a graduate-level context. If your background is in other proof-based courses (but not analysis): work through all components carefully. The quantifier-negation exercises and the induction worked example are especially important; do not skip the Practice Problem Set.
