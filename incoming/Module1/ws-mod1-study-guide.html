    Section 2 Module 1: Companion Reading and Study Guide

    How to use this guide. Work through it while reading, not after. Each section names the assignment, explains what to look for, and poses “stop and check” questions. Starred items are especially important for MAT 5610 preparation.
    🔗

    Reading 1: Zorn §1.4 “Proofs 101” (Suggested time: 20 minutes).

    Zorn treats proof-writing as a language skill. He opens with concrete prose rules (use standard notation; define everything; write complete “sentences”; watch word order; make it look easy; etc.) and then builds up a lexicon of logical terms. As you read, identify each rule and be able to say why it matters.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1. Correcting a Malformed Statement.

    Zorn writes that “For all \(\Q\text{,}\) we have \(x^2 \geq 0\)” is nonsensical. Explain why this sentence fails, and write a corrected version.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The statement fails because \(\Q\) denotes a set, not a variable. The quantifier “for all” must bind a variable ranging over a domain, not the domain itself. A corrected version: “For all \(x \in \Q\text{,}\) we have \(x^2 \geq 0\text{.}\)” In symbols: \(\forall x \in \Q,\; x^2 \geq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2. Word Order and Quantifiers.

    Zorn’s “Watch word order” section gives two statements involving \(\varepsilon\) and \(n\) that look similar but have opposite truth values. Write out both statements, and explain why their quantifier order makes them different. (This pair recurs in Module 2 on sequences.)
    🔗
    Answer.
    The two statements (over \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) and \(n \in \N\)) are:
    1. \(\forall \varepsilon \gt 0 \;\; \exists n \in \N \text{ such that } \tfrac{1}{n} \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\) True (Archimedean property).
      🔗
    2. \(\exists n \in \N \text{ such that } \forall \varepsilon \gt 0,\;\; \tfrac{1}{n} \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\) False: no fixed \(n\) makes \(1/n\) smaller than every positive \(\varepsilon\text{.}\)
      🔗
    The difference is which variable is fixed first. In (a) \(n\) may depend on \(\varepsilon\text{;}\) in (b) \(n\) must work for all \(\varepsilon\) simultaneously.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3. Scratch Work vs. Finished Proof.

    The “Make it look easy” rule says a finished proof should hide the scratch work. Does this mean you should not show your reasoning? Explain the distinction Zorn is drawing.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Zorn is not saying to omit reasoning; he is saying that the finished proof should read as a clean, forward-moving argument rather than a record of how you found the proof. Scratch work (trying cases, working backwards, computing examples) is essential privately, but the write-up presents the logical chain in order, without dead ends or self-corrections. In MAT 5610, assessments will ask you to include clearly labeled scratch work before the formal proof.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.4. De Morgan’s Laws for Quantifiers.

    State De Morgan’s laws for quantified statements (not just for “and”/“or”). Use the symbols \(\forall\text{,}\) \(\exists\text{,}\) and \(\neg\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    The quantifier De Morgan laws (where \(P(x)\) is a predicate over domain \(D\)):
    \begin{align*} \neg\bigl(\forall x \in D,\; P(x)\bigr) \amp\iff \exists x \in D,\; \neg P(x)\\ \neg\bigl(\exists x \in D,\; P(x)\bigr) \amp\iff \forall x \in D,\; \neg P(x) \end{align*}
    In words: the negation of a universal is an existential counterexample; the negation of an existential is a universal denial.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5. Archimedean Property in Symbols.

    Zorn gives an example of the Archimedean property using quantifiers. Write it out in full symbolic form and explain in one sentence what it means. (You will prove this in Module 2.)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\forall x \in \R,\; \exists n \in \N \text{ such that } x \lt n\text{.}\) This says: every real number is exceeded by some natural number, i.e., the natural numbers are unbounded above in \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.6. Converse and Contrapositive.

    For the implication “If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\text{,}\) then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\)”:
    1. State the converse. Is it true?
      🔗
    2. State the contrapositive. Is it true?
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    1. Converse: “If \(f\) is continuous at \(a\text{,}\) then \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\text{.}\)” False. The function \(f(x) = |x|\) is continuous at \(0\) but not differentiable there.
      🔗
    2. Contrapositive: “If \(f\) is not continuous at \(a\text{,}\) then \(f\) is not differentiable at \(a\text{.}\)” True (logically equivalent to the original implication, which is a standard theorem).
      🔗

    Checkpoint 2.7. Irrationality of \(\sqrt{5}\).

    Zorn proves that \(\sqrt{5}\) is irrational using an argument about prime factorizations (rather than the parity argument). In your own words, describe the key idea. What property of squaring makes the argument work?
    🔗
    Answer.
    The proof assumes \(\sqrt{5} = p/q\) in lowest terms, so \(p^2 = 5q^2\text{.}\) Since 5 is prime, \(5 \mid p^2\) implies \(5 \mid p\text{,}\) so write \(p = 5k\text{;}\) then \(25k^2 = 5q^2\text{,}\) giving \(5 \mid q\text{.}\) This contradicts \(\gcd(p,q)=1\text{.}\) Squaring preserves prime divisibility: if \(p \mid a^2\) and \(p\) is prime, then \(p \mid a\) (by unique factorization).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.8. Infinitely Many Primes.

    Zorn proves there are infinitely many primes by contradiction. Trace through the proof: what is the assumption, what contradicting object is constructed, and what property of that object creates the contradiction?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Assumption: There are finitely many primes, \(p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_k\text{.}\) Constructed object: \(N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k + 1\text{.}\) Contradiction: Every prime \(p_i\) divides \(p_1 \cdots p_k\text{,}\) so \(p_i \nmid N\) (it leaves remainder 1). But \(N \gt 1\) must have some prime factor—a prime not on our list. This contradicts the assumption that the list was complete.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.9. Induction: Subsets.

    Zorn’s Claim VIII: a set with \(n\) elements has \(2^n\) subsets. Identify the base case, the induction hypothesis, and the key idea in the inductive step. (Hint: adding one element to a set does something predictable to the number of subsets.)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Base case: \(n = 0\text{.}\) A set with 0 elements has exactly 1 subset (the empty set), and \(2^0 = 1\text{.}\) ✓
    🔗
    Induction hypothesis: Assume any set with \(n\) elements has \(2^n\) subsets.
    🔗
    Inductive step: Let \(A\) have \(n+1\) elements; pick any element \(a \in A\text{.}\) Every subset of \(A\) either contains \(a\) or does not. The subsets not containing \(a\) are exactly the subsets of \(A \setminus \{a\}\)—by the induction hypothesis, there are \(2^n\) of these. The subsets containing \(a\) are obtained by adding \(a\) to each subset of \(A \setminus \{a\}\)—also \(2^n\text{.}\) Total: \(2 \cdot 2^n = 2^{n+1}\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.10. Exhaustive Case Analysis.

    Zorn’s section “Brute Force, Cases, Exhaustion” is brief. What kind of claim is best handled by exhaustive case analysis? Give one example from mathematics (invented or remembered).
    🔗
    Answer.
    Case analysis works best when the claim naturally splits into a finite number of mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive situations, each of which can be handled individually. Example: “For any integer \(n\text{,}\) \(n^2\) leaves remainder 0 or 1 when divided by 4.” Proof by cases: either \(n\) is even (\(n = 2k\text{,}\) so \(n^2 = 4k^2 \equiv 0\)) or \(n\) is odd (\(n = 2k+1\text{,}\) so \(n^2 = 4k^2+4k+1 \equiv 1\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.11. Predicates vs. Functions.

    In Hirst’s notation, what is the difference between a predicate and a function? Why does this distinction matter for mathematical statements?
    🔗
    Answer.
    A function maps inputs to outputs of the same type as the domain (e.g., \(f(x) = x^2\) maps reals to reals). A predicate maps inputs to truth values—it is a property that a term either satisfies or does not (e.g., \(P(x) \equiv (x \gt 0)\)). The distinction matters because only predicates can appear after \(\forall\) or \(\exists\text{;}\) quantifiers bind variables in predicates, not in function expressions.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.12. Quantifier Order with \(L(x,y)\).

    Hirst gives four statements about \(L(x,y)\) using mixed quantifiers. Write all four, translate each into plain English, and identify which pairs have the same truth value when \(L(x,y)\) means “\(x \lt y\)” over \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    The four statements (with \(L(x,y): x \lt y\) over \(\R\)):
    1. \(\forall x\, \forall y,\; L(x,y)\text{:}\) “Every real is less than every real.” False.
      🔗
    2. \(\forall x\, \exists y,\; L(x,y)\text{:}\) “Every real has some real greater than it.” True.
      🔗
    3. \(\exists x\, \forall y,\; L(x,y)\text{:}\) “Some real is less than every real.” False.
      🔗
    4. \(\exists x\, \exists y,\; L(x,y)\text{:}\) “There exist two reals with the first less than the second.” True.
      🔗
    Statements (b) and (d) are both true; (a) and (c) are both false.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.13. Free and Bound Variables.

    In the formula
    \begin{equation*} \forall x \bigl(P(x, y) \to \forall y(R(x, y) \vee Q(y))\bigr) \wedge B(x), \end{equation*}
    identify all free and bound variable occurrences. Is this formula a sentence (closed formula)?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Bound occurrences: The outermost \(\forall x\) binds the \(x\) in \(P(x,y)\) and \(R(x,y)\) within the first conjunct. The inner \(\forall y\) binds the \(y\) in \(R(x,y)\) and \(Q(y)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Free occurrences: The \(y\) in \(P(x,y)\) is free (it lies outside the scope of \(\forall y\)). The \(x\) in \(B(x)\) in the second conjunct is free (it lies outside the scope of \(\forall x\)).
    🔗
    Because both \(x\) (in \(B(x)\)) and \(y\) (in \(P(x,y)\)) have free occurrences, this formula is not a sentence; it is an open formula.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.14. Other Strategies.

    Hirst §3.6 lists strategies for proving existence, uniqueness, iff statements, and equalities. Summarize each strategy in one sentence.
    🔗
    Answer.
    1. Existence: Exhibit a specific object that satisfies the required property, or use a non-constructive argument (e.g., contradiction) to show one must exist.
      🔗
    2. Uniqueness: Assume two objects \(a\) and \(b\) both satisfy the property and show \(a = b\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. Iff (biconditional): Prove both directions separately—\(P \Rightarrow Q\) and \(Q \Rightarrow P\)—each by any valid method.
      🔗
    4. Equalities: Show each side is \(\leq\) the other (the “double inequality” method), or transform one side into the other by a chain of equalities.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.15. Contradiction vs. Contrapositive.

    Hirst gives “If \(n^2\) is even, then \(n\) is even” as an example of proof by contradiction. But as Hirst notes, the same result follows from the contrapositive of a direct result. Which approach do you find more natural, and why?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Both are valid. Contrapositive: Assume \(n\) is odd, so \(n = 2k+1\text{;}\) then \(n^2 = 4k^2+4k+1\) is odd. This is clean and direct. Contradiction: Assume \(n^2\) is even but \(n\) is odd; the same calculation shows \(n^2\) is odd, contradiction.
    🔗
    Many analysts prefer the contrapositive here because it avoids the extra negation overhead; contradiction is more natural when there is no obvious “converse” direction to assume. Either approach is acceptable in MAT 5610.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.16. Proving a Conjunction.

    According to Hirst §3.6, to prove \(C \to (A \wedge B)\) directly, what must you show? What does the contrapositive look like for this type of statement?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Direct: Assuming \(C\text{,}\) prove both \(A\) and \(B\) separately. Since the conclusion is a conjunction, you must establish each conjunct under the hypothesis.
    🔗
    Contrapositive: \(\neg(A \wedge B) \to \neg C\text{,}\) which by De Morgan is \((\neg A \vee \neg B) \to \neg C\text{.}\) In practice this is proved by cases: assume \(\neg A\) (or \(\neg B\)) and derive \(\neg C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Synthesis: The Convergence Definition.

    Read the following definition carefully. You will encounter it on the first day of MAT 5610.
    🔗
    Definition (Convergence of a Sequence, Bauldry Ch. 2). A sequence \((a_n)_{n=1}^\infty\) of real numbers converges to a limit \(L \in \R\) if and only if
    \begin{equation*} \forall \varepsilon \gt 0 \;\; \exists N \in \N \text{ such that } n \geq N \implies |a_n - L| \lt \varepsilon. \end{equation*}
    We write \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L\) or \(a_n \to L\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.17. Parsing the Definition.

    Identify the quantifiers, their order, and the universe of each variable in the convergence definition.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The definition contains two quantifiers in this order:
    1. \(\forall \varepsilon \gt 0\text{:}\) universal quantifier over positive reals (\(\varepsilon \in (0,\infty) \subset \R\)).
      🔗
    2. \(\exists N \in \N\text{:}\) existential quantifier over the natural numbers. Note that \(N\) is allowed to depend on \(\varepsilon\) (it comes after the universal).
      🔗
    The free variables already bound at the outer level are \((a_n)\) (the sequence) and \(L\) (the proposed limit). The bound variables are \(\varepsilon\text{,}\) \(N\text{,}\) and (implicitly) \(n\) in \(n \geq N \implies |a_n - L| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.18. Negating the Definition.

    Write the negation of the convergence definition (i.e., the definition of \(a_n \not\to L\)). What does each quantifier become?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Applying the quantifier De Morgan laws:
    \begin{equation*} \exists \varepsilon \gt 0 \;\; \forall N \in \N,\;\; \exists n \geq N \text{ such that } |a_n - L| \geq \varepsilon. \end{equation*}
    Each quantifier flips: \(\forall \varepsilon\) becomes \(\exists \varepsilon\text{;}\) \(\exists N\) becomes \(\forall N\text{;}\) and the implication \(n \geq N \Rightarrow |a_n-L| \lt \varepsilon\) negates to \(\exists n \geq N\) with \(|a_n - L| \geq \varepsilon\text{.}\) In words: the sequence does not converge to \(L\) if there is some \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) such that no matter how far out you go, you can find a term at least \(\varepsilon\) away from \(L\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.19. Formulating Divergence.

    The definition says a sequence converges to a specific limit \(L\text{.}\) Suppose you want to state that a sequence diverges (does not converge to any limit). How would you formulate this using your quantifier rules? (Hint: which symbol does \(L\) become?)
    🔗
    Answer.
    A sequence \((a_n)\) diverges if it converges to no limit, i.e.:
    \begin{equation*} \forall L \in \R,\;\; \exists \varepsilon \gt 0 \;\; \forall N \in \N,\;\; \exists n \geq N \text{ such that } |a_n - L| \geq \varepsilon. \end{equation*}
    The formerly-free variable \(L\) becomes universally quantified at the outermost level: for every candidate limit, the sequence fails to converge to it.
