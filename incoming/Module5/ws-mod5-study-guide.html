    Section 2 Module 5: Companion Reading and Study Guide

    This guide accompanies two sections of Zorn’s Understanding Real Analysis. Section 2.5 (“Series 101: Basic Ideas”) introduces the formal definition of series convergence via partial sums, the geometric series, and the basic algebra of convergent series. Section 2.6 (“Series 102: Testing for Convergence”) develops the named tests: Comparison, Limit Comparison, Ratio, Root, and Alternating Series, along with a brief treatment of absolute convergence.
    🔗
    Read each section before working its questions. Because many of the tests may be familiar from a calculus course, pay particular attention to the statements and proofs—not just the recipes. The recurring theme, which you should be able to articulate by the end of this guide, is that every test is ultimately a statement about the partial-sum sequence \((S_n)\) or (for absolute convergence) \((T_n) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} |a_k|\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Section 2.5: Series 101 — Basic Ideas (Zorn pp. 119–134).

    Read Definition 2.22 (series convergence) very carefully. This is the definition on which all of Module 5 rests. Notice that it is not a new kind of limit—it is the old Module 4 limit, applied to the partial-sum sequence.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1. Parsing the Definition.

    Write Zorn’s Definition 2.22 (convergence of a series) symbolically. Your statement should involve the partial sums \(S_n = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n\) and the \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) definition of sequence convergence from Module 4.
    🔗
    1. What is the type of the object \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k\text{?}\) (Is it a number? A sequence? A symbol for a limit?)
      🔗
    2. If asked to prove “\(\sum a_k\) converges to \(S\)”, which specific sequence must you show converges to \(S\text{,}\) and by what definition?
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) When the series converges, \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k\) denotes the real number \(\lim_{n \to \infty} S_n\text{.}\) When it diverges, the symbol has no numerical value (though we sometimes write \(\sum a_k = \infty\) loosely for divergence to infinity). (b) You must show that the partial-sum sequence \((S_n)\) converges to \(S\) in the \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) sense: \(\forall \varepsilon \gt 0\ \exists N \in \N\ \forall n \geq N: |S_n - S| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2. The Geometric Series Computation.

    Zorn derives the formula
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=1}^{n} ar^{k-1} = a \cdot \frac{1 - r^n}{1 - r} \quad (r \neq 1), \end{equation*}
    and then takes \(n \to \infty\) to evaluate the infinite sum when \(|r| \lt 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Prove the closed-form partial-sum identity by induction on \(n\text{.}\) (The base case is \(n = 1\text{.}\))
      🔗
    2. Explain why \(r^n \to 0\) when \(|r| \lt 1\text{.}\) (You do not need to prove this rigorously, but you should be able to point at the Module 4 tool that does the work; in Zorn it appears as Theorem 2.16 or near Proposition 2.24.)
      🔗
    3. Conclude: when \(|r| \lt 1\text{,}\) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} ar^{k-1} = \frac{a}{1 - r}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) Base: \(n = 1\) gives \(a = a(1 - r)/(1 - r)\text{.}\) Induction step: if the formula holds for \(n\text{,}\) add \(ar^n\) to both sides and simplify to get the formula with \(n + 1\) in place of \(n\text{.}\) (b) For \(|r| \lt 1\text{,}\) the sequence \((|r|^n)\) is decreasing and bounded below by 0; MCT gives a limit \(L \geq 0\text{.}\) Since \(|r|^{n+1} = |r| \cdot |r|^n\text{,}\) passing to the limit on both sides gives \(L = |r| \cdot L\text{,}\) so \(L (1 - |r|) = 0\text{,}\) so \(L = 0\text{.}\) (c) Using the sum and quotient rules from the Algebra of Limits together with \(r^n \to 0\) gives \(a(1 - r^n)/(1 - r) \to a \cdot 1/(1 - r)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3. The Divergence Test as a Corollary of Module 4.

    Read Zorn’s statement of the Divergence Test (the “nth-term test”): if \(\sum a_k\) converges, then \(a_k \to 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Prove this from scratch, using the definition of series convergence and a Module 4 limit law of your choice.
    🔗
    (Hint: \(a_n = S_n - S_{n-1}\text{.}\))
    🔗
    Answer.
    Assume \(\sum a_k\) converges, so \(S_n \to S\) for some \(S \in \R\text{.}\) The sequence \((S_{n-1})\) is obtained by shifting \((S_n)\) back by one index; every subsequence of \((S_n)\) has the same limit, so \(S_{n-1} \to S\) as well. By the difference rule (Algebra of Limits), \(a_n = S_n - S_{n-1} \to S - S = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.4. The Harmonic Series as Counterexample.

    The converse of the Divergence Test is false: there exist series with \(a_k \to 0\) that still diverge. Zorn emphasizes the harmonic series \(\sum 1/k\) as the standard example.
    🔗
    Reproduce one of the proofs that \(\sum 1/k\) diverges. The cleanest is the classical grouping argument (sometimes attributed to Oresme in the 14th century):
    \begin{equation*} H_{2^m} = 1 + \frac{1}{2} + \left(\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{5} + \cdots + \frac{1}{8}\right) + \cdots \end{equation*}
    Show that each parenthesized block is bounded below by \(1/2\text{,}\) and conclude that \(H_{2^m} \geq 1 + m/2\text{,}\) so \(H_{2^m} \to \infty\text{,}\) hence \((H_n)\) diverges.
    🔗
    Answer.
    In the block \(\frac{1}{2^k + 1} + \frac{1}{2^k + 2} + \cdots + \frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\text{,}\) each of the \(2^k\) summands is at least \(1/2^{k+1}\text{,}\) so the block is at least \(2^k \cdot 1/2^{k+1} = 1/2\text{.}\) Summing \(m\) such blocks, \(H_{2^m} \geq 1 + m/2\text{.}\) Since \(m/2 \to \infty\text{,}\) so does \(H_{2^m}\text{,}\) and the harmonic series diverges.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5. Algebra with Convergent Series.

    Read Zorn’s Theorem 2.23 (algebra with convergent series). State the results for sums and constant multiples, and explain in one sentence why each is a direct consequence of the Algebra of Limits from Module 4 applied to partial sums.
    🔗
    Answer.
    If \(\sum a_n = A\) and \(\sum b_n = B\text{,}\) then \(\sum (a_n \pm b_n) = A \pm B\) and \(\sum c a_n = c A\) for any constant \(c\text{.}\) Each follows by applying the sum rule (resp. constant-multiple rule) from Module 4 to the partial-sum sequences \((S_n^{(a)})\) and \((S_n^{(b)})\text{:}\) the partial sum of \(a_n + b_n\) is \(S_n^{(a)} + S_n^{(b)}\text{,}\) and limits of sums are sums of limits.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Section 2.6: Series 102 — Testing for Convergence (Zorn pp. 134–146).

    Section 2.6 is a catalog of convergence tests. Read slowly, because this is where the recipes get proved. For each test, identify: the hypothesis on the terms, the conclusion about the series, and the Module 4 fact (MCT, comparison of bounded sequences, geometric decay) that drives the proof.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.6. The Comparison Test.

    State Zorn’s Comparison Test (Theorem 2.26 or its analogue): if \(0 \leq a_k \leq b_k\) for all \(k\text{,}\) then (i) \(\sum b_k\) converges \(\Rightarrow\) \(\sum a_k\) converges, and (ii) \(\sum a_k\) diverges \(\Rightarrow\) \(\sum b_k\) diverges.
    🔗
    Then answer:
    1. Why is (i) the contrapositive of (ii)?
      🔗
    2. In the proof of (i), where is the Monotone Convergence Theorem used, and what object does it produce?
      🔗
    3. Why is the hypothesis \(a_k, b_k \geq 0\) essential? What could go wrong if the terms could be negative?
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) Direct restatement: \(P \Rightarrow Q\) and \(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P\) are logically equivalent. (b) The partial sums \(S_n^{(a)}\) and \(S_n^{(b)}\) are both increasing (because terms are nonnegative). If \(\sum b_k = B\text{,}\) then \(S_n^{(b)} \leq B\) for all \(n\text{,}\) so \(S_n^{(a)} \leq S_n^{(b)} \leq B\text{,}\) so \((S_n^{(a)})\) is increasing and bounded above. MCT delivers a limit. (c) Without nonnegativity, \((S_n^{(a)})\) need not be monotone, so MCT does not apply; moreover the inequality \(a_k \leq b_k\) by itself does not prevent partial sums from oscillating wildly.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.7. The Limit Comparison Test.

    State Zorn’s Limit Comparison Test (Theorem 2.29): for series \(\sum a_k\) and \(\sum b_k\) with positive terms, if \(\lim_{k \to \infty} a_k / b_k = L \in (0, \infty)\text{,}\) then \(\sum a_k\) and \(\sum b_k\) either both converge or both diverge.
    🔗
    Apply the test to determine whether \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k^2 + k}\) converges, by comparison with \(\sum 1/k^2\text{.}\) (You may take \(\sum 1/k^2\) as a known convergent series; Zorn proves this both directly and via the \(p\)-series theorem.)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Let \(a_k = 1/(k^2 + k)\) and \(b_k = 1/k^2\text{.}\) Then \(a_k/b_k = k^2/(k^2 + k) = 1/(1 + 1/k) \to 1 \in (0, \infty)\) by the algebra of limits. Since \(\sum 1/k^2\) converges, Limit Comparison implies \(\sum 1/(k^2 + k)\) converges.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.8. The Ratio Test.

    State Zorn’s Ratio Test: for \(\sum a_k\) with positive terms, if \(\lim_{k \to \infty} a_{k+1}/a_k = L\text{,}\) then the series converges if \(L \lt 1\text{,}\) diverges if \(L \gt 1\text{,}\) and the test is inconclusive at \(L = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Sketch the proof of the \(L \lt 1\) case. The idea is to pick any \(r\) with \(L \lt r \lt 1\text{,}\) use the hypothesis to produce an index \(N\) past which \(a_{k+1}/a_k \lt r\text{,}\) and then bound \(a_{N+j} \leq a_N \cdot r^j\text{.}\) Invoke the geometric series.
      🔗
    2. Why does the test fail at \(L = 1\text{?}\) Give one series with \(L = 1\) that converges and one that diverges.
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) Given \(L \lt 1\text{,}\) choose \(r = (L + 1)/2 \in (L, 1)\text{.}\) Pick \(N\) with \(a_{k+1}/a_k \lt r\) for \(k \geq N\text{.}\) Induction gives \(a_{N+j} \lt a_N r^j\text{.}\) Then \(\sum_{k \geq N} a_k \lt a_N \sum_{j \geq 0} r^j = a_N/(1-r) \lt \infty\text{.}\) Comparison with this geometric bound yields convergence of \(\sum a_k\) (the first \(N - 1\) terms are a finite sum and do not affect convergence). (b) Harmonic series \(\sum 1/k\text{:}\) ratio \(k/(k+1) \to 1\text{;}\) diverges. \(p\)-series \(\sum 1/k^2\text{:}\) ratio \(k^2/(k+1)^2 \to 1\text{;}\) converges.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.9. Absolute Convergence.

    Read Zorn’s discussion of absolute convergence. A series \(\sum a_k\) converges absolutely if the series \(\sum |a_k|\) of absolute values converges.
    🔗
    The central theorem is: absolute convergence implies convergence. Outline the proof in two or three sentences. (Hint: use the Cauchy criterion for the partial sums \((S_n)\text{,}\) together with the triangle inequality applied to finite sums.)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Let \(T_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} |a_k|\text{.}\) If \((T_n)\) converges, it is Cauchy: for every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) there exists \(N\) with \(T_n - T_m = \sum_{k=m+1}^{n} |a_k| \lt \varepsilon\) for \(n \gt m \geq N\text{.}\) By the triangle inequality, \(|S_n - S_m| = \left|\sum_{k=m+1}^{n} a_k\right| \leq \sum_{k=m+1}^{n} |a_k| \lt \varepsilon\text{,}\) so \((S_n)\) is Cauchy, and by Cauchy completeness of \(\R\) (Module 4), \((S_n)\) converges.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.10. The Alternating Series Test.

    State the Alternating Series Test: if \((b_k)\) is a positive, decreasing sequence with \(b_k \to 0\text{,}\) then \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{k+1} b_k\) converges.
    🔗
    Read Zorn’s proof and answer:
    1. What is the sequence of even partial sums, \((S_{2m})\text{?}\) Is it monotone, and bounded? Conclusion?
      🔗
    2. Same question for the odd partial sums \((S_{2m+1})\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. Why do the two subsequences have the same limit, and why does that imply the full sequence \((S_n)\) converges?
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) \(S_{2m} = (b_1 - b_2) + (b_3 - b_4) + \cdots + (b_{2m-1} - b_{2m})\text{;}\) each group is nonnegative, so \((S_{2m})\) is increasing. Also \(S_{2m} = b_1 - (b_2 - b_3) - \cdots - (b_{2m-2} - b_{2m-1}) - b_{2m} \leq b_1\text{,}\) so bounded above. MCT gives a limit \(L\text{.}\) (b) \(S_{2m+1} = S_{2m} + b_{2m+1}\text{;}\) since \(b_{2m+1} \to 0\text{,}\) \(S_{2m+1} \to L\) as well. Directly: \((S_{2m+1})\) is decreasing and bounded below by 0, also by MCT. (c) Both subsequences have limit \(L\text{;}\) since every term of \((S_n)\) is either an even or odd partial sum, and both subsequences converge to \(L\text{,}\) the whole sequence does. (Formally: for any \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) pick \(N\) so that both \(|S_{2m} - L|\) and \(|S_{2m+1} - L|\) are less than \(\varepsilon\) for \(2m, 2m+1 \geq N\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.11. Reading Ahead: The Integral Test.

    Zorn §2.6 (or §2.5 problem set) mentions the Integral Test: if \(f(x) \geq 0\) is continuous and decreasing on \([1, \infty)\text{,}\) then \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} f(k)\) converges if and only if the improper integral \(\int_1^{\infty} f(x)\,dx\) converges.
    🔗
    Without proving anything: use the Integral Test (informally) to argue that \(\sum 1/k^p\) converges for \(p \gt 1\) and diverges for \(p \leq 1\text{.}\) (You may appeal to calculus-level facts about \(\int_1^{\infty} x^{-p}\,dx\text{.}\))
    🔗
    Note: the Integral Test will not be used in the worked examples or on the module assessment. It is covered here because Bauldry states it in the Bridge Reading, and it foreshadows the precise integral/series comparisons developed in MAT 5610 via the Riemann integral.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\int_1^{\infty} x^{-p}\,dx\) converges if \(p \gt 1\) (to \(1/(p-1)\)) and diverges if \(p \leq 1\text{.}\) So by the Integral Test, \(\sum 1/k^p\) converges exactly when \(p \gt 1\text{.}\) This recovers the \(p\)-series theorem.
