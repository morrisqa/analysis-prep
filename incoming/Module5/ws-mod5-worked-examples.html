    Section 3 Module 5: Worked Examples

    Read each example slowly. The goal is not just to see what the answer is, but to internalize the proof template. Example 1 is the archetype of a direct partial-sums argument (telescoping); Example 2 shows the Limit Comparison Test in action; Example 3 demonstrates the Ratio Test on a factorial series; Example 4 treats conditional convergence via the Alternating Series Test.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1. Example 1: A Telescoping Series from Partial Sums.

    Prove directly from the definition of series convergence that
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k(k+1)} = 1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy. To prove a series equals a specific number, we must show its partial-sum sequence \((S_n)\) converges to that number. Here we will find an explicit closed form for \(S_n\) and then take a limit using Module 4 tools.
    🔗
    Scratch work. The summand admits the partial-fraction decomposition
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{k(k+1)} = \frac{1}{k} - \frac{1}{k+1}, \end{equation*}
    which one checks by combining the right-hand side over a common denominator. The intermediate terms in the partial sum will cancel in pairs.
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Using the partial-fraction identity, the \(n\)th partial sum is
    \begin{align*} S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{k(k+1)} \amp= \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left(\frac{1}{k} - \frac{1}{k+1}\right)\\ \amp= \left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) + \cdots + \left(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1}\right)\\ \amp= 1 - \frac{1}{n + 1}, \end{align*}
    since all intermediate terms cancel. By the Algebra of Limits (Module 4) together with the standard limit \(1/(n+1) \to 0\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} S_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 - \frac{1}{n + 1}\right) = 1 - 0 = 1. \end{equation*}
    By the definition of series convergence, \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k(k+1)} = 1\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. This is the only example in the module in which the sum is computed exactly from the definition. Two features were critical: (1) a closed form for the summand (partial fractions) that made the cancellation visible, and (2) a closed form for \(S_n\) that reduced the problem to a Module 4 limit. When neither is available—which is nearly always the case—we must use convergence tests to determine whether a limit exists, without being able to identify its value.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2. Example 2: The Limit Comparison Test.

    Determine whether
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k^2 + k} \end{equation*}
    converges. Use the Limit Comparison Test. You may take as known that \(\sum 1/k^2\) converges (this is Zorn’s \(p\)-series theorem with \(p = 2\)).
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy. For large \(k\text{,}\) the summand \(1/(k^2 + k)\) behaves like \(1/k^2\text{,}\) so we compare to \(\sum 1/k^2\text{.}\) The Limit Comparison Test avoids the work of producing an explicit inequality \(a_k \leq b_k\) required by direct comparison.
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Set \(a_k = \dfrac{1}{k^2 + k}\) and \(b_k = \dfrac{1}{k^2}\text{.}\) Both are positive for \(k \geq 1\text{.}\) We compute
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a_k}{b_k} = \frac{1/(k^2 + k)}{1/k^2} = \frac{k^2}{k^2 + k} = \frac{1}{1 + 1/k}. \end{equation*}
    By the quotient rule from the Algebra of Limits and the standard limit \(1/k \to 0\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{a_k}{b_k} = \frac{1}{1 + 0} = 1. \end{equation*}
    Since the limit is finite and nonzero, the Limit Comparison Test applies: \(\sum a_k\) and \(\sum b_k\) either both converge or both diverge. Because \(\sum 1/k^2\) converges, so does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k^2 + k}\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. The rhythm of the proof is standard: (1) identify a “known” series with similar-looking large-\(k\) behavior; (2) compute the ratio of summands and take its limit; (3) verify the limit lies in \((0, \infty)\text{;}\) (4) invoke the test by name. If the ratio limit had been \(0\) or \(\infty\text{,}\) a weaker conclusion would follow (see Zorn’s exact statement of Theorem 2.29), but the case of a finite nonzero limit is the most commonly useful. The hypothesis of positive terms must be checked: Limit Comparison is a test for positive series, so we note explicitly that \(a_k, b_k \gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3. Example 3: The Ratio Test on a Factorial Series.

    Determine whether
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k!}{k^k} \end{equation*}
    converges. Use the Ratio Test.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy. Series involving factorials are almost always attacked with the Ratio Test, because the factorial and exponential ratios simplify cleanly. We compute \(\lim_{k \to \infty} a_{k+1}/a_k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Let \(a_k = k!/k^k \gt 0\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} \amp= \frac{(k+1)!}{(k+1)^{k+1}} \cdot \frac{k^k}{k!}\\ \amp= \frac{(k+1) \cdot k!}{(k+1) \cdot (k+1)^k} \cdot \frac{k^k}{k!}\\ \amp= \frac{k^k}{(k+1)^k} = \left(\frac{k}{k+1}\right)^k = \frac{1}{\left(1 + \frac{1}{k}\right)^k}. \end{align*}
    It is a standard limit (proved in Zorn §2.3 or its problem set; also a limit students know from calculus) that \(\left(1 + \frac{1}{k}\right)^k \to e\) as \(k \to \infty\text{.}\) By the quotient rule,
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.368. \end{equation*}
    Since \(1/e \lt 1\text{,}\) the Ratio Test implies \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k!}{k^k}\) converges. \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. The algebraic simplification in the second step is the whole game: \((k+1)!/k! = k+1\text{,}\) and \((k+1)^{k+1}/(k+1)^k = k+1\text{,}\) so a single factor of \((k+1)\) in numerator and denominator cancels and leaves a clean ratio. Recognizing the \((1 + 1/k)^k\) pattern—rather than fumbling with binomial expansions—is what makes the test feel routine. The Ratio Test delivers a yes/no verdict only; it does not tell you what the sum is. For \(\sum k!/k^k\text{,}\) there is no known closed form for the sum.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4. Example 4: Conditional Convergence via the Alternating Series Test.

    Prove that
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{\sqrt{k}} \end{equation*}
    converges, and then show that it does not converge absolutely. Conclude that the series converges conditionally.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy. Absolute convergence is the stronger condition, so we handle it in stages. First, use the Alternating Series Test to get convergence. Then, look at \(\sum |a_k| = \sum 1/\sqrt{k}\) and show it diverges via comparison with the harmonic series (or directly as a \(p\)-series with \(p = 1/2 \lt 1\)).
    🔗
    Step 1: Convergence via AST.
    🔗
    Write the series as \(\sum (-1)^{k+1} b_k\) with \(b_k = 1/\sqrt{k} \gt 0\text{.}\) To apply the Alternating Series Test we verify two conditions:
    🔗
    (i) \((b_k)\) is decreasing. For \(k \in \N\text{,}\) \(\sqrt{k} \lt \sqrt{k+1}\text{,}\) so \(1/\sqrt{k} \gt 1/\sqrt{k+1}\text{,}\) i.e., \(b_k \gt b_{k+1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (ii) \(b_k \to 0\text{.}\) For any \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) by the Archimedean Property pick \(N \gt 1/\varepsilon^2\text{.}\) Then for \(k \geq N\text{,}\) \(\sqrt{k} \geq \sqrt{N} \gt 1/\varepsilon\text{,}\) so \(b_k = 1/\sqrt{k} \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Both hypotheses of the Alternating Series Test are satisfied, so \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{\sqrt{k}}\) converges.
    🔗
    Step 2: Absolute divergence.
    🔗
    Consider the series of absolute values, \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\text{.}\) We show this diverges by direct comparison with the harmonic series. Since \(\sqrt{k} \leq k\) for all \(k \in \N\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{k} \leq \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}. \end{equation*}
    The harmonic series \(\sum 1/k\) diverges (Study Guide Exercise 4 of §2.5). By the contrapositive form of the Comparison Test, \(\sum 1/\sqrt{k}\) also diverges.
    🔗
    Conclusion.
    🔗
    The series \(\sum (-1)^{k+1}/\sqrt{k}\) converges (Step 1) but not absolutely (Step 2). By definition, it converges conditionally. \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. The proof template for classifying an alternating series has three parts: (1) apply AST to establish convergence, carefully verifying both hypotheses on \(b_k\text{;}\) (2) check absolute convergence separately, usually via comparison with a known series; (3) declare whether the convergence is absolute or conditional. The order matters: you cannot conclude conditional convergence from AST alone—you must also rule out absolute convergence. Conditional convergence is a delicate property: rearranging the terms of a conditionally convergent series can change its sum (Riemann’s rearrangement theorem, a topic for MAT 5610).
