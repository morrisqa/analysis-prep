    Section 5 Module 5: Bridge Reading Guide

    Every module ends with a bridge reading in Bauldry’s Introduction to Real Analysis. For this module the reading covers two sections: §1.5 (“Sequences and Series of Constants”) and §1.6 (“Power Series and Taylor Series”). Both sit in Bauldry’s informal Chapter 1, which reviews elementary calculus with a gentle lean toward proof. The rigorous redevelopment of sequences and series happens in Bauldry §2.5—which is the starting point of MAT 5610.
    🔗
    Treat §1.5 as a compressed review: Bauldry lists every test in a few pages, with examples but without full proofs. Treat §1.6 as a preview: power series and Taylor series are topics you saw in calculus; reading them in Bauldry’s style sets you up for Module 9, where sequences and series of functions (uniform convergence, power series) take center stage.
    🔗
    For this module, read: Bauldry §1.5 (“Sequences and Series of Constants”, approximately pp. 25–30) and §1.6 (“Power Series and Taylor Series”, approximately pp. 31–36).
    🔗

    Bauldry §1.5 — Sequences and Series of Constants.

    Bauldry’s §1.5 opens with sequences (Definition 1.13, Theorem 1.32 “Algebra of Sequence Limits”) and then pivots to series via partial sums. The section lists the named tests—Ratio, Root, Comparison, Integral, Alternating—in rapid succession as Theorems 1.35–1.39. Unlike Zorn, who proves each test in detail, Bauldry mostly states them and refers to exercises for proofs.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.1. Series as a “Special Sequence”.

    Read Bauldry’s definition of a series (it begins “A series \(\{s_n\}\) is a special sequence…” and defines \(s_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k\)).
    1. Quote Bauldry’s one-sentence definition precisely.
      🔗
    2. Notice that Bauldry treats the partial-sum sequence as the primary object—he writes \(\{s_n\}\text{,}\) not \(\sum a_k\text{,}\) for the series. How does this align with the framing of Video 1 (“a series is a sequence in disguise”)?
      🔗
    3. Bauldry’s notation \(\{s_n\}\) makes it typographically obvious that convergence of the series is convergence of a sequence. What does this suggest about the role of §1.5 in setting up §2.5 (the rigorous treatment)?
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.2. Example 1.11: Special Series.

    Read Example 1.11 (“Special Series of Elementary Calculus”), which catalogs geometric, harmonic, \(p\)-series, and telescoping series as a reference set.
    1. For the two telescoping series Bauldry lists (\(\sum 1/(k(k+1))\) and \(\sum 1/((2k-1)(2k+1))\)), are the closed-form partial sums Bauldry gives consistent with your Worked Example 1 and Practice Set S1? (They should be: 1 and 1/2 respectively.)
      🔗
    2. Bauldry credits Oresme (14th century) with the harmonic series divergence argument. Reconstruct the argument from Bauldry’s exposition and identify the central idea. (It is the grouping argument from Study Guide §2.5 Ex. 4.)
      🔗

    Checkpoint 5.3. The Catalog of Tests (Theorems 1.35–1.39).

    Bauldry states five convergence tests in quick succession: Ratio (Thm 1.35), Root (Thm 1.36), Comparison (Thm 1.37), Integral (Thm 1.38), Alternating Series (Thm 1.39).
    🔗
    1. For each of Ratio, Root, Comparison, and Alternating: is Bauldry’s statement consistent with Zorn’s? Note one small difference in phrasing you notice (e.g., whether \(r = \lim a_{n+1}/a_n\) is assumed to exist, or replaced by \(\limsup\text{;}\) whether “positive” or “nonnegative” terms are assumed).
      🔗
    2. Bauldry’s Integral Test (Thm 1.38) is the one named test you have not seen proved in this module’s Study Guide or Worked Examples. State the hypotheses precisely: \(f\) must be continuous, positive, and decreasing on \([1, \infty)\text{.}\) Why are each of these hypotheses required?
      🔗
    3. Bauldry remarks that the Alternating Series Test has a useful remainder bound: the tail \(\sum_{k > n} (-1)^{k-1} a_k\) is bounded in absolute value by \(a_{n+1}\text{.}\) Why is this plausible? (Think about the squeezing of even and odd partial sums between consecutive \(a_k\text{.}\))
      🔗

    Bauldry §1.6 — Power Series and Taylor Series.

    Section 1.6 extends the idea of a series by letting the terms depend on a variable \(x\text{:}\) a power series centered at \(c\) is an expression
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n (x - c)^n. \end{equation*}
    For a fixed value of \(x\text{,}\) this is just a series of numbers, and the Module 5 tests apply. For \(x\) ranging over an interval, we get a function defined wherever the series converges. This is the subject of Module 9.
    🔗
    A preview, not a full treatment. The questions below are designed to help you read §1.6 for orientation, not to master it. Skim for the main ideas; you will return to them when power series reappear in the rigorous setting.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.4. Definition of a Power Series.

    Quote Bauldry’s Definition 1.17 (“Power Series”).
    1. For a fixed value of \(x\text{,}\) what kind of object is \(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n (x - c)^n\text{,}\) and which of the Module 5 tests could you, in principle, apply to decide whether it converges?
      🔗
    2. The radius of convergence (Definition 1.18) is the key summary statistic for a power series. State the three possibilities: \(R = 0\) (converges only at \(x = c\)), \(R = \infty\) (converges for all \(x\)), and \(0 \lt R \lt \infty\) (converges for \(|x - c| \lt R\text{,}\) diverges for \(|x - c| \gt R\)).
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.5. Finding the Radius of Convergence via the Ratio Test.

    Bauldry’s Example 1.14 uses the Ratio Test on the Maclaurin series for \(e^x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}. \end{equation*}
    1. Reproduce the ratio-test computation: the ratio of consecutive terms (in absolute value) is \(|x^{n+1}/(n+1)!| \cdot |n!/x^n| = |x|/(n+1)\text{,}\) which tends to 0 for every fixed \(x \in \R\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Conclude the interval of convergence: the series converges for every \(x \in \R\text{,}\) so the radius of convergence is \(R = \infty\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. Now apply the same technique to the geometric series \(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n\text{.}\) What ratio do you compute, and what radius of convergence do you obtain? (You should recover \(R = 1\text{.}\))
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (c) Ratio \(= |x^{n+1}|/|x^n| = |x|\text{.}\) The Ratio Test says the series converges when \(|x| \lt 1\text{,}\) diverges when \(|x| \gt 1\text{,}\) and is inconclusive at \(|x| = 1\text{.}\) Hence \(R = 1\text{.}\) (At \(x = \pm 1\) the series diverges for reasons outside the Ratio Test—at \(x = 1\text{,}\) \(a_k = 1 \not\to 0\text{;}\) at \(x = -1\text{,}\) the alternating partial sums oscillate.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 5.6. What Gets Harder in the Functional Setting.

    Bauldry’s Theorem 1.40 states that a convergent power series can be differentiated and integrated term by term inside its radius of convergence.
    🔗
    Read the theorem and answer, in one or two sentences each:
    1. Why is term-by-term differentiation of an infinite sum a nontrivial operation? (Think about what “sum” means for an infinite series.)
      🔗
    2. Bauldry does not prove Theorem 1.40 in §1.6. Module 9 of this course (on sequences and series of functions) will develop the tool required to prove it: uniform convergence. Without reading ahead, make a guess: why might “pointwise convergence for each \(x\)” not be enough to justify switching a derivative with an infinite sum?
      🔗
