    Section 2 Module 6: Companion Reading and Study Guide

    This guide accompanies four sections of Zorn’s Understanding Real Analysis. Section 3.1 (“Limits of Functions”) introduces the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) definition and the sequential characterization of limits. Sections 3.2 and 3.3 develop the theory of continuous functions, including the Intermediate Value Theorem and Extreme Value Theorem. Section 3.4 introduces uniform continuity and the Heine–Cantor theorem.
    🔗
    As you read, keep in mind the central structural theme: the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) definition is a direct analogue of the \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) definition from Module 4. Every theorem about limits of functions has a counterpart for sequences, and the sequential characterization (Theorem 3.3 or its analogue in Zorn) is the bridge that lets you use Module 4 tools here.
    🔗

    Section 3.1: Limits of Functions (Zorn pp. 151–164).

    Read the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) definition of \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) carefully. Compare it line by line with the \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) definition of sequence convergence from Module 4. The structural parallel is the key takeaway from this section.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1. Parsing the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) Definition.

    Write the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) definition of \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) symbolically. Then answer:
    1. What is the geometric meaning of the condition \(0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta\text{?}\) In particular, why does \(0 \lt |x - a|\) (rather than \(|x - a| \lt \delta\) alone) appear?
      🔗
    2. In the sequence definition, the condition was “\(n \geq N\)”. What is the analogue here, and what is the role of \(\delta\) compared to \(N\text{?}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    The definition: \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) means \(\forall \varepsilon \gt 0\ \exists \delta \gt 0\) such that \(0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta \Rightarrow |f(x) - L| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) The condition \(0 \lt |x - a| \lt \delta\) describes a punctured open interval around \(a\text{:}\) \(x\) is within \(\delta\) of \(a\) but not equal to \(a\text{.}\) The strict inequality \(0 \lt |x - a|\) is essential because the limit concerns the behavior of \(f\) near \(a\text{,}\) not at \(a\text{;}\) in particular, \(f\) need not even be defined at \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) In Module 4, \(N\) is a threshold index past which the sequence stays within \(\varepsilon\) of \(L\text{.}\) Here, \(\delta\) plays the same role: it is a threshold distance from \(a\) within which \(f(x)\) stays within \(\varepsilon\) of \(L\text{.}\) The two definitions have exactly parallel logical structure.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2. A Basic \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) Verification.

    Prove from the definition that \(\lim_{x \to 3} (2x - 1) = 5\text{.}\) Show your scratch work (finding \(\delta\)) separately from the formal proof.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Scratch work. \(|f(x) - 5| = |2x - 1 - 5| = |2x - 6| = 2|x - 3|\text{.}\) For this to be less than \(\varepsilon\text{,}\) we need \(|x - 3| \lt \varepsilon / 2\text{.}\) Set \(\delta = \varepsilon / 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof. Let \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{.}\) Set \(\delta = \varepsilon / 2\text{.}\) Suppose \(0 \lt |x - 3| \lt \delta\text{.}\) Then \(|f(x) - 5| = 2|x - 3| \lt 2\delta = \varepsilon\text{.}\) By definition, \(\lim_{x \to 3} (2x - 1) = 5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3. Sequential Characterization of Limits.

    Read Zorn’s sequential characterization of limits (sometimes called the Heine criterion): \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) if and only if for every sequence \((x_n)\) with \(x_n \to a\) and \(x_n \neq a\) for all \(n\text{,}\) we have \(f(x_n) \to L\text{.}\)
    1. Explain in one sentence how this theorem connects Module 4 to Module 6.
      🔗
    2. Use the sequential characterization to argue that \(\lim_{x \to 0} \sin(1/x)\) does not exist. (Hint: find two sequences approaching 0 along which the function values approach different values.)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) The sequential characterization reduces questions about function limits to questions about sequence limits, so every Module 4 tool (Algebra of Limits, Squeeze Theorem, etc.) applies directly to function limits via this bridge.
    🔗
    (b) Let \(x_n = 1/(2\pi n)\) and \(y_n = 1/(\pi/2 + 2\pi n)\text{.}\) Both satisfy \(x_n \to 0\) and \(y_n \to 0\) with \(x_n \neq 0\) and \(y_n \neq 0\text{.}\) We have \(\sin(1/x_n) = \sin(2\pi n) = 0 \to 0\) and \(\sin(1/y_n) = \sin(\pi/2 + 2\pi n) = 1 \to 1\text{.}\) Since two sequences approaching 0 give different function values, the sequential characterization implies the limit does not exist.
    🔗
    🔗

    Section 3.2 defines continuity at a point and on a set, and develops the algebra of continuous functions. Section 3.3 proves the Intermediate Value Theorem and Extreme Value Theorem. Section 3.4 introduces uniform continuity and the Heine–Cantor theorem. Read all three before working the exercises below.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.4. Continuity at a Point.

    Write the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) definition of continuity of \(f\) at \(a\text{.}\) Then prove that \(f(x) = x^2\) is continuous at \(a = 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (Hint: \(|x^2 - 9| = |x - 3||x + 3|\text{.}\) Restrict \(|x - 3| \lt 1\) first to bound \(|x + 3|\text{,}\) then set \(\delta = \min(1, \varepsilon / 7)\text{.}\))
    🔗
    Answer.
    Definition. \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) if \(\forall \varepsilon \gt 0\ \exists \delta \gt 0\) such that \(|x - a| \lt \delta \Rightarrow |f(x) - f(a)| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\) (Note: unlike the limit definition, we do not need \(0 \lt |x - a|\text{,}\) because continuity includes the requirement that \(f(a)\) is the right value.)
    🔗
    Scratch work. \(|x^2 - 9| = |x - 3||x + 3|\text{.}\) If \(|x - 3| \lt 1\) then \(|x| \lt 4\text{,}\) so \(|x + 3| \leq |x| + 3 \lt 7\text{.}\) So \(|x^2 - 9| \lt 7|x - 3|\text{.}\) Set \(\delta = \min(1, \varepsilon/7)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof. Let \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{.}\) Set \(\delta = \min(1, \varepsilon/7)\text{.}\) Suppose \(|x - 3| \lt \delta\text{.}\) Since \(\delta \leq 1\text{,}\) we have \(|x - 3| \lt 1\text{,}\) so \(|x + 3| \lt 7\text{.}\) Thus \(|x^2 - 9| = |x - 3||x + 3| \lt \delta \cdot 7 \leq \varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5. Intermediate Value Theorem.

    State the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT). Then use it to show that the equation \(x^3 + x - 1 = 0\) has at least one solution in the interval \((0, 1)\text{.}\)
    1. Identify the function \(f\) and verify it is continuous on \([0, 1]\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Compute \(f(0)\) and \(f(1)\) and verify they have opposite signs.
      🔗
    3. Invoke the IVT by name to conclude.
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    IVT. If \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and \(f(a) \lt 0 \lt f(b)\) (or \(f(a) \gt 0 \gt f(b)\)), then there exists \(c \in (a, b)\) with \(f(c) = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Let \(f(x) = x^3 + x - 1\text{.}\) As a polynomial, \(f\) is continuous everywhere, hence on \([0, 1]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) \(f(0) = 0 + 0 - 1 = -1 \lt 0\) and \(f(1) = 1 + 1 - 1 = 1 \gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) Since \(f\) is continuous on \([0, 1]\) with \(f(0) \lt 0 \lt f(1)\text{,}\) the Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees a \(c \in (0, 1)\) with \(f(c) = 0\text{,}\) i.e., \(c^3 + c - 1 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.6. Extreme Value Theorem and Compactness.

    State the Extreme Value Theorem (EVT). Then give an example showing the EVT can fail if the interval is not closed and bounded.
    1. Let \(f(x) = 1/x\) on \((0, 1]\text{.}\) Does \(f\) attain a maximum value? Does it attain a minimum?
      🔗
    2. What hypothesis of the EVT fails for this example?
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    EVT. If \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) (a closed bounded interval), then there exist \(c, d \in [a, b]\) with \(f(c) \leq f(x) \leq f(d)\) for all \(x \in [a, b]\text{.}\) That is, \(f\) attains both its minimum and maximum values.
    🔗
    (a) \(f(x) = 1/x\) on \((0, 1]\) has minimum value \(f(1) = 1\) (attained) but no maximum: as \(x \to 0^+\text{,}\) \(f(x) \to +\infty\text{,}\) so \(f\) is unbounded above.
    🔗
    (b) The interval \((0, 1]\) is not closed (it does not contain its left endpoint 0), violating the hypothesis of the EVT.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.7. Definition of Uniform Continuity.

    Write the definition of uniform continuity of \(f\) on a set \(S\text{.}\) Then prove that \(f(x) = 2x + 1\) is uniformly continuous on \(\R\text{.}\)
    1. Identify the key difference in quantifier order between the definition of pointwise continuity on \(S\) and the definition of uniform continuity on \(S\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Prove uniform continuity of \(f(x) = 2x + 1\) on \(\R\) by finding \(\delta\) in terms of \(\varepsilon\) only (not depending on any point \(x\) or \(y\)).
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Definition. \(f\) is uniformly continuous on \(S\) if \(\forall \varepsilon \gt 0\ \exists \delta \gt 0\) such that for all \(x, y \in S\) with \(|x - y| \lt \delta\text{,}\) we have \(|f(x) - f(y)| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) In pointwise continuity on \(S\text{,}\) the definition reads: for all \(x \in S\text{,}\) for all \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) there exists \(\delta \gt 0\) (which may depend on both \(\varepsilon\) and \(x\)) such that \(|y - x| \lt \delta \Rightarrow |f(y) - f(x)| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\) In uniform continuity, the quantifier \(\forall x \in S\) is moved inside the \(\exists \delta\text{:}\) for all \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) there exists \(\delta \gt 0\) (depending only on \(\varepsilon\)) such that for all \(x, y \in S\) with \(|x - y| \lt \delta\text{,}\) we have \(|f(x) - f(y)| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) \(|f(x) - f(y)| = |(2x + 1) - (2y + 1)| = 2|x - y|\text{.}\) Given \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) set \(\delta = \varepsilon / 2\text{.}\) Then for any \(x, y \in \R\) with \(|x - y| \lt \delta\text{,}\) we get \(|f(x) - f(y)| = 2|x - y| \lt 2\delta = \varepsilon\text{.}\) The same \(\delta\) works for all \(x, y\text{,}\) so \(f\) is uniformly continuous.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.8. Failure of Uniform Continuity.

    Show that \(f(x) = 1/x\) is not uniformly continuous on \((0, 1)\text{.}\) Use the sequential criterion for non-uniform continuity: find sequences \((x_n)\) and \((y_n)\) in \((0, 1)\) with \(|x_n - y_n| \to 0\) but \(|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| \not\to 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Let \(x_n = 1/n\) and \(y_n = 1/(n + 1)\text{.}\) Both lie in \((0, 1)\) for \(n \geq 2\text{.}\) We have
    \begin{equation*} |x_n - y_n| = \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1} = \frac{1}{n(n+1)} \to 0. \end{equation*}
    But
    \begin{equation*} |f(x_n) - f(y_n)| = |n - (n+1)| = 1 \end{equation*}
    for all \(n\text{.}\) Since \(|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| = 1 \not\to 0\text{,}\) the definition of uniform continuity fails for \(\varepsilon = 1/2\) (say): no single \(\delta\) can prevent \(|f(x) - f(y)|\) from exceeding \(1/2\) for pairs \(x, y\) near 0.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.9. Heine–Cantor Theorem.

    Read Zorn’s statement of the Heine–Cantor theorem: if \(f\) is continuous on a closed bounded interval \([a, b]\text{,}\) then \(f\) is uniformly continuous on \([a, b]\text{.}\)
    1. Why does the theorem require closed and bounded rather than just bounded? (Think about what fails for \(f(x) = 1/x\) on \((0, 1]\text{.}\))
      🔗
    2. Why does the theorem require continuity on all of \([a, b]\) rather than just continuity at each interior point?
      🔗
    3. The proof of Heine–Cantor uses the theory of compact sets and open covers. Zorn may prove it differently. Identify the key property of \([a, b]\) that the proof uses, and note that this is a topic developed fully in MAT 5610.
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) \(f(x) = 1/x\) is continuous on \((0, 1]\) but not uniformly continuous (Exercise sg-34-2): the interval is bounded but not closed (0 is missing), and the function blows up near the missing endpoint.
    🔗
    (b) If continuity fails at even one point in \([a, b]\text{,}\) the function may have a jump discontinuity, which would prevent uniform continuity near that point.
    🔗
    (c) The key property is compactness of \([a, b]\text{:}\) every open cover has a finite subcover (Heine–Borel theorem). The proof extracts a finite cover from the family of \(\delta\)-neighborhoods that work pointwise, then takes the minimum of the finitely many \(\delta\) values. Compactness is developed rigorously in MAT 5610.
