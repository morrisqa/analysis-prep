    Section 6 Module 8: Assessment

    This assessment has three parts. Parts 1 and 2 ask you to write complete mathematical proofs, submitted for feedback. Part 3 is a short reflection. Submit all three parts together as a single document (PDF or typed file) through the course management system.
    🔗
    Write-up standard. All proofs must be written in complete mathematical prose. Use the style modeled in the Worked Examples: announce your strategy, include scratch work (clearly labeled) before the formal proof, write in complete grammatical sentences, invoke each theorem by name, and justify every step. Work consisting only of symbolic manipulations without explanation will not receive full credit.
    🔗
    A note on Part 2. Part 2 is a single required proof problem; there are no options to choose between. All students complete the same Part 2 problem.
    🔗
    Academic integrity. Your proofs must be your own work. You may refer to your notes, the textbooks, and the worked examples from this module. You may not collaborate with other students on the written proofs or use AI writing tools to draft your arguments.
    🔗
    Part 1: Riemann Integrability from the Definition (Required) (Estimated time: 15 minutes)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.1. Proving a Constant Function is Integrable.

    Prove that \(f(x) = c\) (a constant) is Riemann integrable on \([a,b]\) and \(\int_a^b c\,dx = c(b-a)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Your proof must have the following structure:
    1. Setup. Let \(P\) be any partition of \([a,b]\) with \(n\) subintervals. Compute \(M_k = \sup f\) on \([x_{k-1}, x_k]\) and \(m_k = \inf f\) on \([x_{k-1}, x_k]\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Compute upper and lower sums. Show that \(U(f,P) = c(b-a)\) and \(L(f,P) = c(b-a)\) for every partition \(P\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. Apply the criterion. Conclude that \(U(f,P) - L(f,P) = 0 \lt \varepsilon\) for every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) and every partition \(P\text{,}\) so the integrability criterion is satisfied.
      🔗
    4. Identify the integral. Since all upper and lower sums equal \(c(b-a)\text{,}\) conclude that the upper and lower integrals both equal \(c(b-a)\text{,}\) and hence \(\int_a^b c\,dx = c(b-a)\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    This is the simplest possible integrability proof: since \(f\) is identically \(c\text{,}\) the supremum and infimum of \(f\) on every subinterval are both equal to \(c\text{.}\) Therefore \(U = L\) for every partition, which is a much stronger condition than the integrability criterion requires.
    🔗
    🔗

    Part 2: Applying the Fundamental Theorem (Required) (Estimated time: 20 minutes)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2. FTC in Action.

    This problem has two parts.
    1. Applying FTC Part 2. State FTC Part 2 precisely, listing all three hypotheses (continuity of \(f\text{;}\) existence of an antiderivative \(F\text{;}\) the relation \(F' = f\)). Then evaluate \(\int_1^e (1/x)\,dx\) by: exhibiting an antiderivative \(F(x)\) of \(f(x) = 1/x\text{;}\) verifying that \(F'(x) = 1/x\text{;}\) verifying that \(f\) is continuous on \([1, e]\text{;}\) and applying FTC Part 2 to compute \(F(e) - F(1)\text{.}\) Show every step.
      🔗
    2. Applying FTC Part 1 with the chain rule. State FTC Part 1 precisely. Then compute \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\int_1^{x^3} \sin(t^2)\,dt\text{.}\) Identify the antiderivative function \(G(u) = \int_1^u \sin(t^2)\,dt\text{,}\) apply FTC Part 1 to find \(G'(u)\text{,}\) and apply the chain rule to compute the derivative of \(G(x^3)\) with respect to \(x\text{.}\) Show every step.
      🔗

    Part 3: Reflection (Estimated time: 5 minutes)
    🔗
    Write 3–5 sentences for each question. These are graded for thoughtfulness, not for mathematical correctness.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.3. Uniform Continuity and Integrability.

    Explain in your own words, in 3–5 sentences, how uniform continuity (Module 6) appears in the proof that continuous functions are Riemann integrable. What specific role does it play, and could the proof work with only pointwise continuity?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.4. Bauldry §2.4 — First Impressions.

    In the Bridge Reading you encountered the Riemann-Stieltjes integral. In 3–5 sentences, describe what is new in the Stieltjes setting compared to the Riemann integral. What does the integrator \(\alpha\) add, and why might this generalization be useful?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.5. FTC as a Bridge.

    The FTC connects differentiation (Module 7) and integration (Module 8). In 2–3 sentences, describe this connection in your own words. Which direction — Part 1 (differentiating an integral gives the integrand) or Part 2 (integrating a derivative gives the boundary values) — do you find more surprising, and why?
