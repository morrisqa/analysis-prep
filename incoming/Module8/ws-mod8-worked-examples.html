    Section 3 Module 8: Worked Examples

    Read each example slowly. The goal is to internalize the proof template, not just to see the answer. Example 1 is the archetype of a direct Darboux argument; Example 2 shows the proof that continuous implies integrable; Example 3 proves FTC Part 1 in full; Example 4 shows FTC Part 2 in a computation.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1. Example 1: \(f(x) = x\) is Riemann Integrable on \([0,1]\) and \(\int = 1/2\).

    Prove from the Darboux definition that \(f(x) = x\) is Riemann integrable on \([0,1]\) and \(\int_0^1 x\,dx = 1/2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy.
    🔗
    Use uniform partitions \(P_n\) with \(n\) equal subintervals, compute upper and lower sums explicitly, show their difference tends to zero, and use the integrability criterion. The common limit of \(U(f,P_n)\) and \(L(f,P_n)\) will identify the integral.
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Let \(P_n\) be the uniform partition with points \(x_k = k/n\) for \(k = 0, 1, \ldots, n\text{,}\) so each subinterval has width \(\Delta x = 1/n\text{.}\) Since \(f(x) = x\) is increasing, on the \(k\)th subinterval \([(k-1)/n, k/n]\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} M_k = \sup f = \frac{k}{n}, \qquad m_k = \inf f = \frac{k-1}{n}. \end{equation*}
    The upper and lower sums are:
    \begin{align*} U(f,P_n) \amp= \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{k}{n} \cdot \frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot \frac{n(n+1)}{2} = \frac{n+1}{2n},\\ L(f,P_n) \amp= \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{k-1}{n} \cdot \frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{k=1}^{n} (k-1) = \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{n-1}{2n}. \end{align*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} U(f,P_n) - L(f,P_n) = \frac{n+1}{2n} - \frac{n-1}{2n} = \frac{2}{2n} = \frac{1}{n} \to 0. \end{equation*}
    By the integrability criterion, \(f\) is Riemann integrable on \([0,1]\text{.}\) Moreover, since the upper and lower integrals are equal (both squeezed between \(U\) and \(L\)), and
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} U(f,P_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n+1}{2n} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \lim_{n \to \infty} L(f,P_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n-1}{2n} = \frac{1}{2}, \end{equation*}
    the common value of the upper and lower integrals is \(1/2\text{.}\) Hence \(\int_0^1 x\,dx = 1/2\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. This is the simplest possible integrability proof from the definition: the function is increasing, so \(M_k\) and \(m_k\) are just the values at the right and left endpoints. The difference \(U - L = 1/n\) has a beautifully transparent form. In Worked Example 2, the same strategy — bound \(M_i - m_i\) on each subinterval — is applied to continuous functions, where uniform continuity does the bounding.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2. Example 2: Continuous Functions are Riemann Integrable.

    Prove that if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\text{,}\) then \(f\) is Riemann integrable on \([a,b]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy.
    🔗
    Use the Heine-Cantor theorem (uniform continuity of continuous functions on closed bounded intervals, proved in Module 6) to bound the oscillation \(M_i - m_i\) on each subinterval, then sum up and apply the integrability criterion.
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Since \(f\) is continuous on the closed bounded interval \([a,b]\text{,}\) the Heine-Cantor theorem (Module 6) gives that \(f\) is uniformly continuous on \([a,b]\text{.}\) Let \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) be given. By uniform continuity, there exists \(\delta \gt 0\) such that for all \(x, y \in [a,b]\) with \(|x - y| \lt \delta\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} |f(x) - f(y)| \lt \frac{\varepsilon}{b - a}. \end{equation*}
    Choose any partition \(P = \{x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n\}\) of \([a,b]\) with \(\|P\| \lt \delta\) (such a partition exists; take, for instance, a uniform partition with \(n \gt (b-a)/\delta\)).
    🔗
    On each subinterval \([x_{i-1}, x_i]\text{,}\) any two points \(x, y\) satisfy \(|x - y| \leq \|P\| \lt \delta\text{,}\) so \(|f(x) - f(y)| \lt \varepsilon/(b-a)\text{.}\) Taking the supremum over \(x\) and infimum over \(y\) (or vice versa) gives \(M_i - m_i \leq \varepsilon/(b-a)\text{.}\) Therefore:
    \begin{equation*} U(f,P) - L(f,P) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (M_i - m_i)\,\Delta x_i \leq \frac{\varepsilon}{b-a} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Delta x_i = \frac{\varepsilon}{b-a} \cdot (b-a) = \varepsilon. \end{equation*}
    Since \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) was arbitrary and we exhibited a partition \(P\) with \(U(f,P) - L(f,P) \lt \varepsilon\text{,}\) the integrability criterion is satisfied. Hence \(f\) is Riemann integrable on \([a,b]\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. Uniform continuity is indispensable: the same \(\delta\) must work at every point of \([a,b]\) simultaneously, which is exactly the guarantee uniform continuity provides. Pointwise continuity gives a different \(\delta\) at each point, which could tend to zero and prevent us from finding a single partition that works. The proof template is: (1) invoke Heine-Cantor; (2) choose a partition with mesh smaller than \(\delta\text{;}\) (3) bound \(M_i - m_i\text{;}\) (4) sum up; (5) invoke the criterion.

    Checkpoint 3.3. Example 3: FTC Part 1.

    Prove FTC Part 1: if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt\) for \(x \in [a,b]\text{,}\) then \(F\) is differentiable and \(F'(x) = f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy.
    🔗
    Compute \((F(x+h) - F(x))/h\) using the additivity of the integral, and show that it approaches \(f(x)\) as \(h \to 0\text{.}\) The key is that \(f(x)\) can be written as the average value of the constant \(f(x)\) over \([x, x+h]\text{,}\) so the difference quotient minus \(f(x)\) is controlled by how close \(f(t)\) is to \(f(x)\) for \(t\) near \(x\) — which is exactly what continuity of \(f\) at \(x\) gives.
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Fix \(x \in (a,b)\) and consider \(h \neq 0\) small enough that \(x + h \in [a,b]\text{.}\) By the additivity of the integral,
    \begin{equation*} F(x+h) - F(x) = \int_a^{x+h} f(t)\,dt - \int_a^x f(t)\,dt = \int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,dt \end{equation*}
    (for \(h \gt 0\text{;}\) the case \(h \lt 0\) is analogous). Dividing by \(h\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{F(x+h) - F(x)}{h} = \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,dt. \end{equation*}
    Since \(f(x)\) is constant with respect to \(t\text{,}\) \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(x)\,dt\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \frac{F(x+h) - F(x)}{h} - f(x) = \frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} [f(t) - f(x)]\,dt. \end{equation*}
    Let \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{.}\) Since \(f\) is continuous at \(x\text{,}\) there exists \(\delta \gt 0\) such that \(|t - x| \lt \delta \Rightarrow |f(t) - f(x)| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\) For \(|h| \lt \delta\) and \(t\) between \(x\) and \(x + h\text{,}\) we have \(|t - x| \leq |h| \lt \delta\text{,}\) so \(|f(t) - f(x)| \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\) Therefore:
    \begin{equation*} \left|\frac{F(x+h) - F(x)}{h} - f(x)\right| = \left|\frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h}[f(t) - f(x)]\,dt\right| \leq \frac{1}{|h|} \cdot |h| \cdot \varepsilon = \varepsilon. \end{equation*}
    Since \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) was arbitrary, the limit of the difference quotient is \(f(x)\text{,}\) i.e., \(F'(x) = f(x)\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. The proof has two moving parts: the additivity identity \(\int_a^{x+h} - \int_a^x = \int_x^{x+h}\text{,}\) and the continuity estimate that makes \(|f(t) - f(x)|\) small when \(|t - x|\) is small. The bound \(|(1/h)\int_x^{x+h} g\,dt| \leq \sup|g|\) (averaging an estimate over a small interval preserves the estimate) is the analytic core of the argument.

    Checkpoint 3.4. Example 4: A Computation Using FTC.

    Compute \(\int_0^{\pi} \sin(x)\,dx\) using FTC Part 2.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy.
    🔗
    Find an antiderivative \(F\) with \(F'(x) = \sin(x)\text{,}\) verify that \(f(x) = \sin(x)\) is continuous on \([0, \pi]\text{,}\) and apply FTC Part 2.
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Take \(F(x) = -\cos(x)\text{.}\) We verify: \(F'(x) = \sin(x)\) (by the standard differentiation formula, proved in Module 7 or calculus). The function \(f(x) = \sin(x)\) is continuous on \([0, \pi]\) (it is continuous everywhere). Since \(F'(x) = \sin(x) = f(x)\) on \([0, \pi]\text{,}\) FTC Part 2 applies:
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{\pi} \sin(x)\,dx = F(\pi) - F(0) = -\cos(\pi) - (-\cos(0)) = -(-1) - (-1) = 1 + 1 = 2. \end{equation*}
    \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. The proof template for applying FTC Part 2 has three steps: (1) exhibit an antiderivative \(F\) and verify \(F' = f\text{;}\) (2) check that \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) (the hypothesis of FTC Part 2); (3) compute \(F(b) - F(a)\text{.}\) Step (2) is often omitted in calculus courses but is essential for the theorem to apply. In this example, \(\sin\) is continuous everywhere, so there is nothing to check, but for a piecewise-defined function the continuity condition would require verification.
