    Section 6 Module 9: Assessment

    This assessment has three parts. Parts 1 and 2 ask you to write complete mathematical proofs, submitted for feedback. Part 3 is a short reflection. Submit all three parts together as a single document (PDF or typed file) through the course management system.
    🔗
    Write-up standard. All proofs must be written in complete mathematical prose. Use the style modeled in the Worked Examples: announce your strategy, include scratch work (clearly labeled) before the formal proof, write in complete grammatical sentences, invoke each theorem by name, and justify every step. Work consisting only of symbolic manipulations without explanation will not receive full credit.
    🔗
    A note on Part 2. Part 2 is a single required proof problem; there are no options to choose between. All students complete the same Part 2 problem.
    🔗
    Academic integrity. Your proofs must be your own work. You may refer to your notes, the textbooks, and the worked examples from this module. You may not collaborate with other students on the written proofs or use AI writing tools to draft your arguments.
    🔗
    Part 1: Uniform Convergence Preserves Continuity (Required) (Estimated time: 15 minutes)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.1. The Continuity Theorem.

    Prove the following theorem: If \(f_n \to f\) uniformly on \([a,b]\) and each \(f_n\) is continuous on \([a,b]\text{,}\) then \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Your proof must have the following structure:
    1. Setup. Fix \(x_0 \in [a,b]\) and \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{.}\) State what you need to show and identify the three quantities you will bound.
      🔗
    2. Choose \(N\) from uniform convergence. Use the definition of uniform convergence to obtain \(N \in \N\) such that \(|f_n(x) - f(x)| \lt \varepsilon/3\) for all \(n \geq N\) and all \(x \in [a,b]\text{.}\) Specify the value of \(N\) you fix.
      🔗
    3. Choose \(\delta\) from continuity of \(f_N\text{.}\) Use continuity of \(f_N\) at \(x_0\) to obtain \(\delta \gt 0\) such that \(|x - x_0| \lt \delta \Rightarrow |f_N(x) - f_N(x_0)| \lt \varepsilon/3\text{.}\)
      🔗
    4. The triangle inequality. Suppose \(|x - x_0| \lt \delta\text{.}\) Write out the \(3\varepsilon\) split
      \begin{equation*} |f(x) - f(x_0)| \leq |f(x) - f_N(x)| + |f_N(x) - f_N(x_0)| + |f_N(x_0) - f(x_0)| \end{equation*}
      and bound each term by \(\varepsilon/3\text{,}\) citing the source of each bound.
      🔗
    5. Conclusion. State why \(f\) is continuous at \(x_0\text{,}\) and why this implies \(f\) is continuous on all of \([a,b]\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    The critical step is (ii): the \(N\) you obtain from uniform convergence must not depend on \(x\)—it works simultaneously for all \(x \in [a,b]\text{.}\) This is exactly the content of the quantifier structure of uniform convergence, and it is what makes the proof work. If you find yourself choosing a different \(N\) for each \(x\text{,}\) you are using only pointwise convergence.

    Part 2: Analyzing a Non-Uniform Sequence (Required) (Estimated time: 20 minutes)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2. The Sequence \(f_n(x) = nx\,e^{-nx}\).

    Let \(f_n(x) = nx\,e^{-nx}\) for \(x \in [0,1]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Pointwise limit. Compute \(\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x)\) for each fixed \(x \in [0,1]\text{.}\) (Handle \(x = 0\) and \(x \in (0,1]\) separately.) Call the pointwise limit \(f(x)\) and state it explicitly.
      🔗
    2. Supremum of \(|f_n - f|\text{.}\) For each \(n\text{,}\) find the maximum of \(|f_n(x) - f(x)|\) over \(x \in [0,1]\text{.}\) (Use single-variable calculus: differentiate, set equal to zero, check the critical point.) Compute \(\sup_{x \in [0,1]} |f_n(x) - f(x)|\) and determine whether this supremum tends to \(0\) as \(n \to \infty\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. Uniform convergence verdict. Does \(f_n \to f\) uniformly on \([0,1]\text{?}\) Justify your answer using the result of (ii).
      🔗
    4. Comparing integrals. Compute \(\displaystyle\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_0^1 f_n(x)\,dx\) and \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx\text{.}\) Are they equal? What does the discrepancy (if any) illustrate about the relationship between pointwise convergence and interchange of limits and integrals?
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    For part (i): recall that \(nx \to \infty\) as \(n \to \infty\) when \(x \gt 0\text{,}\) so you need \(u e^{-u} \to 0\) as \(u \to \infty\)—which follows from L’Hôpital’s rule or the standard limit \(e^u \gg u\text{.}\) For part (ii): differentiate \(g(x) = nx\,e^{-nx}\) with respect to \(x\) and set \(g'(x) = 0\text{.}\) For part (iv): use integration by parts \(\int_0^1 nx\,e^{-nx}\,dx\text{.}\)

    Part 3: Reflection (Estimated time: 5 minutes)
    🔗
    Write 3–5 sentences for each question. These are graded for thoughtfulness, not for mathematical correctness.

    Checkpoint 6.3. The Quantifier Swap.

    The only formal difference between pointwise and uniform convergence is the order of two quantifiers. In 3–5 sentences, explain in your own words why this single quantifier swap has such dramatic consequences—why does moving \(\exists N\) outside the \(\forall x\) change what properties the limit function inherits?
