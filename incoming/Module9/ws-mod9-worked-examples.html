    Section 3 Module 9: Worked Examples

    Read each example slowly. Example 1 dissects the canonical non-uniform example \(f_n(x) = x^n\text{.}\) Example 2 proves the uniform-limit continuity theorem using the \(3\varepsilon\) trick. Example 3 applies the Weierstrass \(M\)-Test. Example 4 uses term-by-term integration to derive a classical series identity.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1. Example 1: Pointwise but Not Uniform Convergence.

    Let \(f_n(x) = x^n\) for \(x \in [0,1]\text{.}\) Show that \((f_n)\) converges pointwise but not uniformly on \([0,1]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy. For pointwise convergence, fix \(x\) and compute \(\lim_{n \to \infty} x^n\text{.}\) For non-uniform convergence, compute the sup-norm \(\sup_{x \in [0,1]} |f_n(x) - f(x)|\) and show it does not go to \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Pointwise limit.
    🔗
    For \(x \in [0,1)\text{:}\) \(|x| \lt 1\text{,}\) so \(x^n \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\text{.}\) For \(x = 1\text{:}\) \(1^n = 1\) for all \(n\text{,}\) so \(x^n \to 1\text{.}\) Define \(f(x) = 0\) for \(x \in [0,1)\) and \(f(1) = 1\text{.}\) Then \(f_n \to f\) pointwise on \([0,1]\text{.}\) Note that \(f\) is discontinuous at \(x = 1\text{,}\) even though each \(f_n\) is continuous on \([0,1]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Non-uniform convergence (sup-norm argument).
    🔗
    For any \(n \in \N\) and any \(x \in [0,1)\text{,}\) \(|f_n(x) - f(x)| = x^n\text{.}\) As \(x \to 1^-\text{,}\) \(x^n \to 1\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \sup_{x \in [0,1]} |f_n(x) - f(x)| = 1 \end{equation*}
    for every \(n\text{.}\) (The sup is not attained in \([0,1)\) but equals \(1\) since the function \(x \mapsto x^n\) approaches \(1\) as \(x \to 1^-\text{;}\) at \(x=1\text{,}\) \(|f_n(1)-f(1)|=0\text{,}\) so the sup over \([0,1]\) is still \(1\) by continuity of \(x^n\) on \([0,1)\text{.}\)) Since the sup is \(1\) for every \(n\text{,}\) it does not converge to \(0\text{,}\) and the convergence is not uniform. \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. The failure of uniform convergence here has a visible consequence: the pointwise limit is discontinuous. The theorem in Example 2 says this cannot happen under uniform convergence of continuous functions. The non-uniform behavior of \(x^n\) on \([0,1]\) is the prototypical counterexample for this entire module.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.2. Example 2: Uniform Convergence Preserves Continuity.

    Prove: if \(f_n \to f\) uniformly on \([a,b]\) and each \(f_n\) is continuous on \([a,b]\text{,}\) then \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy. Fix \(x_0 \in [a,b]\) and \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{.}\) Split \(|f(x) - f(x_0)|\) into three pieces using the triangle inequality, and control each piece separately.
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Since \(f_n \to f\) uniformly, there exists \(N \in \N\) such that for all \(n \geq N\) and all \(x \in [a,b]\text{,}\) \(|f_n(x) - f(x)| \lt \varepsilon/3\text{.}\) Fix such an \(N\text{;}\) the function \(f_N\) is continuous at \(x_0\text{,}\) so there exists \(\delta \gt 0\) such that \(|x - x_0| \lt \delta \Rightarrow |f_N(x) - f_N(x_0)| \lt \varepsilon/3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now suppose \(|x - x_0| \lt \delta\text{.}\) By the triangle inequality,
    \begin{align*} |f(x) - f(x_0)| \amp \leq |f(x) - f_N(x)| + |f_N(x) - f_N(x_0)| + |f_N(x_0) - f(x_0)|\\ \amp \lt \frac{\varepsilon}{3} + \frac{\varepsilon}{3} + \frac{\varepsilon}{3} = \varepsilon. \end{align*}
    Since \(x_0\) was arbitrary, \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. This is the \(3\varepsilon\) trick. The three terms are controlled by: (1) uniform convergence applied at \(x\text{;}\) (2) continuity of \(f_N\text{;}\) (3) uniform convergence applied at \(x_0\text{.}\) The key is that \(N\) is chosen before \(x\)—this is exactly what uniform convergence buys. With only pointwise convergence, the \(N\) from (1) would depend on \(x\text{,}\) making the argument circular.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.3. Example 3: The Weierstrass \(M\)-Test.

    Show that \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\sin(nx)}{n^2}\) converges absolutely and uniformly on \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy. Find a bound \(M_n\) independent of \(x\) with \(|f_n(x)| \leq M_n\) and \(\sum M_n \lt \infty\text{.}\) Then invoke the Weierstrass \(M\)-Test.
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    Set \(f_n(x) = \sin(nx)/n^2\text{.}\) For all \(x \in \R\) and all \(n \in \N\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} |f_n(x)| = \frac{|\sin(nx)|}{n^2} \leq \frac{1}{n^2} =: M_n. \end{equation*}
    The series \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} M_n = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2}\) is a convergent \(p\)-series (\(p = 2 \gt 1\)). By the Weierstrass \(M\)-Test, \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \sin(nx)/n^2\) converges absolutely and uniformly on \(\R\text{.}\) \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. The \(M\)-Test is essentially a comparison test for series of functions, with the dominating series \(\sum M_n\) playing the role of the known convergent series. The uniformity of the bound \(|f_n(x)| \leq M_n\) (no \(x\) in \(M_n\)) is what delivers uniform convergence. Notice also that we used \(|\sin(nx)| \leq 1\)—a global bound available precisely because the domain is all of \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.4. Example 4: Term-by-Term Integration.

    Use the geometric series and the term-by-term integration theorem to show that
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1} = \frac{\pi}{4}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Strategy. Start from the geometric series for \(1/(1+t^2)\text{,}\) integrate term by term (justified by uniform convergence on a compact interval), and evaluate at \(x = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Proof.
    🔗
    For \(|t| \lt 1\text{,}\) the geometric series gives
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{1 + t^2} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n t^{2n}. \end{equation*}
    For any \(0 \lt r \lt 1\text{,}\) the series converges uniformly on \([-r, r]\) by the Weierstrass \(M\)-Test (with \(M_n = r^{2n}\) and \(\sum r^{2n} = 1/(1-r^2) \lt \infty\)). Each partial-sum function is continuous, and the uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous (Example 2). By the term-by-term integration theorem, for \(x \in [0,r]\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \int_0^x \frac{dt}{1+t^2} \amp= \int_0^x \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n t^{2n}\,dt = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n \frac{x^{2n+1}}{2n+1}. \end{align*}
    The left side equals \(\arctan(x)\text{.}\) Setting \(x = 1\text{:}\) since the series \(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1}\) converges by the Alternating Series Test (terms positive, decreasing to \(0\)), and \(\arctan(1) = \pi/4\text{,}\) we conclude
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \cdots = \frac{\pi}{4}. \end{equation*}
    \(\square\)
    🔗
    What to notice. The passage from \(x \in [0,r]\) to \(x = 1\) uses a continuity argument (Abel’s theorem) that is beyond this course—we have stated the result but not proved this final step rigorously. The term-by-term integration part is fully rigorous on any \([0,r]\) with \(r \lt 1\text{;}\) extending to the boundary is a topic for MAT 5610. The punchline—\(\pi/4\) as an alternating series of unit fractions—is one of the most beautiful results in elementary analysis.
