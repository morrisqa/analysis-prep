    Section 2 Module 2: Companion Reading and Study Guide

    This guide accompanies three sections of Zorn’s Understanding Real Analysis. Section 1.1 is listed in the module syllabus as background reading: skim it to orient yourself to the number systems and to remind yourself of the proof that \(\sqrt{2} \notin \Q\text{,}\) which is a model contradiction argument. The active reading and guided questions below begin with Section 1.7, where the analytic content starts.
    🔗
    Read each section before working its questions. The questions are designed to be answered with the text open in front of you.
    🔗

    Section 1.7: Absolute Values (Zorn pp. 62–65).

    This section introduces absolute value as a distance function and establishes the properties you will use in every subsequent module. Pay particular attention to Theorem 1.23(c) (which converts an absolute value inequality into an interval) and Theorem 1.24 (the Triangle Inequality).
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1. Absolute Value as Distance.

    For each expression, (i) compute the value and (ii) describe in one sentence what the expression measures geometrically on the number line.
    1. \(\displaystyle |{-3} - 5|\)
      🔗
    2. \(|x - 2|\) where \(x = 7\)
      🔗
    3. \(|x - 2|\) for a general \(x \in \R\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) \(|{-3} - 5| = |{-8}| = 8\text{;}\) this is the distance between \(-3\) and \(5\) on the number line. (b) \(|7 - 2| = 5\text{;}\) the distance from \(7\) to \(2\text{.}\) (c) \(|x - 2|\) is the distance from \(x\) to \(2\text{,}\) regardless of which side of \(2\) the point \(x\) lies on.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2. Converting Absolute Value Inequalities.

    Theorem 1.23(c) says: \(|x| \lt k \iff -k \lt x \lt k\) (for \(k \gt 0\)). Use this to rewrite each inequality as a simple interval, then verify your answer geometrically by identifying the center and radius of the interval.
    1. \(\displaystyle |x - 3| \lt 2\)
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle |x + 1| \leq 4\)
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle |2x - 5| \lt 1\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) \(1 \lt x \lt 5\text{;}\) center \(3\text{,}\) radius \(2\text{.}\) (b) \(-5 \leq x \leq 3\text{;}\) center \(-1\text{,}\) radius \(4\text{.}\) (c) \(|2x - 5| \lt 1 \Rightarrow -1 \lt 2x - 5 \lt 1 \Rightarrow 4 \lt 2x \lt 6 \Rightarrow 2 \lt x \lt 3\text{;}\) center \(5/2\text{,}\) radius \(1/2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3. Key Lemma for the Triangle Inequality.

    One step in Zorn’s proof of the Triangle Inequality uses the fact that for any real number \(x\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} -|x| \leq x \leq |x|. \end{equation*}
    Prove this directly from the definition of absolute value (considering the cases \(x \geq 0\) and \(x \lt 0\) separately).
    🔗
    Hint.
    When \(x \geq 0\text{:}\) \(|x| = x\text{,}\) so the right inequality is \(x \leq x\) (trivially true) and the left inequality is \(-x \leq x\) (true since \(x \geq 0\)). Handle \(x \lt 0\) analogously.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.4. Applying the Triangle Inequality.

    Let \(a, b, c \in \R\text{.}\) Apply the Triangle Inequality to the expression \(a - c = (a - b) + (b - c)\) to obtain an upper bound on \(|a - c|\) in terms of \(|a - b|\) and \(|b - c|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This result is sometimes called the metric inequality for \(\R\text{:}\) the distance from \(a\) to \(c\) is at most the sum of the distances from \(a\) to \(b\) and from \(b\) to \(c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(|a - c| = |(a - b) + (b - c)| \leq |a - b| + |b - c|\) by the Triangle Inequality applied with \(x = a - b\) and \(y = b - c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Section 1.8: Bounds (Zorn pp. 67–73).

    This section introduces the vocabulary of upper and lower bounds and defines the supremum (least upper bound) and infimum (greatest lower bound). Two things to focus on: (1) the definition of supremum requires two conditions, and both matter; (2) the \(\varepsilon\)-characterization of the supremum is the key proof technique you will use repeatedly.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5. Identifying Bounds.

    For each set, determine whether it is bounded above, bounded below, or both. If it is bounded above, give an example of an upper bound (not necessarily the least one).
    1. \(\displaystyle \{n^2 : n \in \N\}\)
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \left\{\dfrac{(-1)^n}{n} : n \in \N\right\}\)
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \{x \in \R : x^2 \leq 9\}\)
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle \left\{1 - \dfrac{1}{n} : n \in \N\right\}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) Bounded below by 1 (or 0); not bounded above. (b) Bounded above and below: all elements satisfy \(|(-1)^n/n| = 1/n \leq 1\text{,}\) so \(-1\) is a lower bound and \(1\) is an upper bound. (c) Bounded above by \(3\) and below by \(-3\text{;}\) this is the interval \([-3, 3]\text{.}\) (d) Bounded above by \(1\) and below by \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.6. Finding Suprema and Infima.

    For each set in the previous exercise that is bounded above, identify \(\sup S\text{.}\) For each set that is bounded below, identify \(\inf S\text{.}\) (Do not prove your answers yet; just identify them and briefly explain your reasoning.)
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) \(\inf\{n^2\} = 1\) (achieved at \(n = 1\)); no supremum. (b) \(\sup = 1/1 = 1\) (at \(n = 1\text{,}\) odd); \(\inf = -1\) (at \(n = 2\text{,}\) even, giving \(-1/2\)—actually the infimum is \(-1/2\) since the most negative term is \((-1)^2/2 = -1/2\)). (c) \(\sup = 3\) and \(\inf = -3\text{,}\) both achieved. (d) \(\sup = 1\) (limit, never achieved); \(\inf = 0\) (at \(n=1\text{,}\) giving \(0\text{,}\) which is achieved).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.7. The Two Conditions for Supremum.

    According to Zorn’s definition, a real number \(s\) equals \(\sup S\) if and only if two conditions hold. State both conditions in your own words (not just symbols), and explain why both are needed. In particular: why is it not enough to say that \(s\) is an upper bound of \(S\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.8. The \(\varepsilon\)-Characterization of the Supremum.

    Zorn states (or you can derive) the following equivalent condition: \(s = \sup S\) if and only if
    1. \(s\) is an upper bound of \(S\text{,}\) and
      🔗
    2. for every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) there exists \(x \in S\) with \(s - \varepsilon \lt x \leq s\text{.}\)
      🔗
    Condition (ii) is called the \(\varepsilon\)-characterization. In plain English, what does condition (ii) say about how \(s\) relates to the elements of \(S\text{?}\) Why does it guarantee that no number smaller than \(s\) can be an upper bound?
    🔗
    🔗

    Section 1.9: Completeness (Zorn pp. 74–82).

    Section 1.9 delivers the central axiom of real analysis. Read this section carefully. The key question to keep in mind is: what would go wrong if we tried to do analysis in \(\Q\) instead of \(\R\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.9. Why \(\Q\) is Incomplete.

    Consider the set \(T = \{x \in \Q : x^2 \lt 2\}\text{.}\)
    1. Explain why \(T\) is nonempty and bounded above (as a subset of \(\Q\)).
      🔗
    2. Explain why \(T\) has no supremum in \(\Q\). (Hint: whatever rational upper bound you propose, you can always find a closer rational that is still above \(\sqrt{2}\)—or use the fact that \(\sqrt{2} \notin \Q\) directly.)
      🔗
    3. Where does the supremum of \(T\) live? What is its value?
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    (a) \(T\) is nonempty: \(1 \in T\) since \(1^2 = 1 \lt 2\text{.}\) It is bounded above in \(\Q\text{:}\) for example, \(2 \in \Q\) and \(2^2 = 4 \gt 2\text{,}\) so \(x \lt 2\) for all \(x \in T\text{.}\) (b) Suppose \(q \in \Q\) were a supremum of \(T\text{.}\) Then \(q^2 \geq 2\) (since \(q\) is an upper bound). But \(q^2 = 2\) would mean \(q = \sqrt{2} \notin \Q\text{,}\) contradiction. And if \(q^2 \gt 2\text{,}\) one can find a rational strictly between \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(q\text{,}\) contradicting \(q\) being the least upper bound. (c) The supremum is \(\sqrt{2} \in \R \setminus \Q\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.10. Stating the Completeness Axiom.

    Write the Completeness Axiom (also called the Least Upper Bound Property) in your own words. Your statement should make clear: what kind of set the axiom applies to, and what the axiom guarantees.
    🔗
    Then check: does your statement apply to the set \(T\) in the previous exercise? What does it tell you?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.11. Completeness and the Archimedean Property.

    Zorn uses the Completeness Axiom to prove the Archimedean Property of \(\R\text{:}\) for every \(x \in \R\text{,}\) there exists \(n \in \N\) with \(n \gt x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Read the proof (or sketch) in Zorn’s text. What set is considered, and what does the Completeness Axiom apply to? Where is the contradiction reached? Write a two- or three-sentence summary of the argument in your own words.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.12. Using the Archimedean Property.

    The Archimedean Property implies the following useful fact: for every \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) there exists \(n \in \N\) such that \(\dfrac{1}{n} \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Explain why this follows from the Archimedean Property. (Hint: apply the Archimedean Property to the real number \(1/\varepsilon\text{.}\))
    🔗
    Why will this fact be useful when we work with sequences in Module 4?
    🔗
    Answer.
    By the Archimedean Property applied to \(x = 1/\varepsilon\text{,}\) there exists \(n \in \N\) with \(n \gt 1/\varepsilon\text{.}\) Since \(n \gt 0\text{,}\) this gives \(1/n \lt \varepsilon\text{.}\) In Module 4, this will allow us to find, for any \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) an index \(N\) beyond which terms of sequences like \(1/n\) lie within \(\varepsilon\) of their limit.
