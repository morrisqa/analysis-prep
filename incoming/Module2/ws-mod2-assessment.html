    Section 6 Module 2: Assessment

    This assessment has three parts. Parts 1 and 2 ask you to write complete mathematical proofs, submitted for feedback. Part 3 is a short reflection. Submit all three parts together as a single document (PDF or typed file) through the course management system.
    🔗
    Write-up standard. All proofs must be written in complete mathematical prose. Use the style modeled in the Worked Examples: include scratch work (clearly labeled) before the formal proof, announce your strategy, write in complete grammatical sentences, and justify every step. Work consisting only of symbolic manipulations without explanation will not receive full credit.
    🔗
    A note on Part 2. All students complete all three parts. For Part 2, choose the option that best matches your background: if you have prior real analysis experience, challenge yourself with Option B. If this is newer territory, Option A is the right starting point.
    🔗
    Academic integrity. Your proofs must be your own work. You may refer to your notes, the textbooks, and the worked examples from this module. You may not collaborate with other students on the written proofs or use AI writing tools to draft your arguments.
    🔗
    Part 1: Core Proof (Required) (Estimated time: 20 minutes)
    🔗
    Consider the set
    \begin{equation*} S = \{x \in \R : x^2 + x \lt 6\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.1. Analyzing the Set.

    Factor the inequality \(x^2 + x - 6 \lt 0\) and determine exactly which real numbers belong to \(S\text{.}\) (That is: what familiar interval is \(S\text{?}\))
    🔗
    Show your algebra clearly. This scratch work does not need to be in formal proof style; it is the analysis step that precedes the proof.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Factor as \((x + 3)(x - 2) \lt 0\) and recall that a product of two real numbers is negative exactly when the factors have opposite signs.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2. Proving the Supremum.

    Using your work in the previous problem, prove that \(\sup S = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Your proof must have the following structure:
    1. Scratch work (clearly labeled). Verify that 2 is an upper bound. Then: given \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) find an explicit element of \(S\) greater than \(2 - \varepsilon\) (for small enough \(\varepsilon\)). Show the algebra.
      🔗
    2. Formal proof. Using your scratch work, write a complete proof that \(\sup S = 2\) by verifying both conditions in the definition of supremum. The proof should be written in complete mathematical prose.
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    For the \(\varepsilon\)-characterization step: you need to show that for any \(\varepsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) the point \(x_0 = 2 - \varepsilon/2\) belongs to \(S\) (at least when \(\varepsilon\) is small). To verify \(x_0 \in S\text{,}\) compute \(x_0^2 + x_0 - 6\) and show it is negative by factoring as \((x_0 + 3)(x_0 - 2)\text{.}\) Note that \(x_0 + 3 \gt 0\) (since \(x_0 \gt -1 \gt -3\) for \(\varepsilon \lt 6\)) and \(x_0 - 2 = -\varepsilon/2 \lt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.3. The Infimum.

    State (without proof) the value of \(\inf S\text{,}\) and write one sentence explaining why the Completeness Axiom guarantees that \(\inf S\) exists in \(\R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Part 2: Proof Choice (Required — Choose One) (Estimated time: 15 minutes)
    🔗
    Choose one of the following two proof problems. Both ask for a complete proof; they differ in difficulty and the techniques required. Label your submission clearly with “Option A” or “Option B.”
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.4. Option A (Standard): Scaling a Supremum.

    Let \(S\) be a nonempty subset of \(\R\) with \(s = \sup S\text{.}\) Let \(c \gt 0\) be a positive real number, and define \(cS = \{cx : x \in S\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Prove that \(\sup(cS) = cs\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Your proof must verify both conditions in the definition of supremum for the set \(cS\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    For condition (i): if \(x \in S\) then \(x \leq s\text{;}\) multiply both sides by \(c \gt 0\text{.}\) For condition (ii): if \(U\) is an upper bound of \(cS\text{,}\) show that \(U/c\) is an upper bound of \(S\) and use \(s \leq U/c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.5. Option B (More Challenging): The Archimedean Property.

    Assuming the Completeness Axiom, prove the Archimedean Property: for every \(x \in \R\text{,}\) there exists \(n \in \N\) such that \(n \gt x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Use proof by contradiction. Your proof should clearly identify: what set the Completeness Axiom is applied to, what supremum is obtained, and what contradiction is derived from the \(\varepsilon\)-characterization.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Suppose no such \(n\) exists; then \(x\) is an upper bound for \(\N\text{.}\) Let \(\alpha = \sup \N\text{.}\) Apply the \(\varepsilon\)-characterization with \(\varepsilon = 1\text{:}\) there exists \(n \in \N\) with \(\alpha - 1 \lt n\text{,}\) so \(n + 1 \gt \alpha\text{.}\) But \(n + 1 \in \N\)…
    🔗
    🔗

    Part 3: Reflection (Estimated time: 5 minutes)
    🔗
    Write 3–5 sentences for each question. These are graded for thoughtfulness, not for mathematical correctness.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.6. The Role of Completeness.

    In your Part 1 proof, where exactly did the Completeness Axiom enter? Was it used explicitly, or did it appear implicitly (for example, in a claim about what \(\sup S\) is)?
    🔗
    Then consider: could you have written the same proof if you were working in \(\Q\) instead of \(\R\text{?}\) Why or why not?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.7. Bauldry’s Proof Structure.

    In the Bridge Reading you read Bauldry’s proof of Theorem 2.3 (Archimedean Property). Compare its logical structure to the proof you wrote in Part 2 (Option B) or the hint you read for Option B. What is the same? If there are differences, where did your proof and Bauldry’s diverge?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.8. Looking Ahead.

    In Module 4, you will prove that sequences like \(a_n = 1 - 1/n\) converge to their limit using an \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) argument. Based on what you practiced in this module, describe the connection you see between the \(\varepsilon\)-characterization of the supremum and the structure of an \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) convergence proof. What is similar? What will be new?
