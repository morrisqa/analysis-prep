    Section 6 Module 3: Assessment

    This assessment has three parts. Parts 1 and 2 ask you to write complete mathematical proofs, submitted for feedback. Part 3 is a short reflection. Submit all three parts together as a single document (PDF or typed file) through the course management system.
    🔗
    Write-up standard. All proofs must be written in complete mathematical prose. Use the style modeled in the Worked Examples: include scratch work (clearly labeled) before the formal proof, announce your strategy, write in complete grammatical sentences, and justify every step. Work consisting only of symbolic manipulations without explanation will not receive full credit.
    🔗
    A note on Part 2. All students complete all three parts. For Part 2, choose the option that best matches your background: if you have prior real analysis experience, challenge yourself with Option B. If this is newer territory, Option A is the right starting point.
    🔗
    Academic integrity. Your proofs must be your own work. You may refer to your notes, the textbooks, and the worked examples from this module. You may not collaborate with other students on the written proofs or use AI writing tools to draft your arguments.
    🔗
    Part 1: Core Proof (Required) (Estimated time: 15 minutes)
    🔗
    The following set equality is one of De Morgan’s laws for set difference. It is structurally similar to Worked Example 1, but involves a different operation and requires a different case analysis.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.1. De Morgan’s Law for Set Difference.

    Let \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) and \(C\) be sets. Prove:
    \begin{equation*} A \setminus (B \cap C) = (A \setminus B) \cup (A \setminus C). \end{equation*}
    Your proof must use the element-chasing method: show each set is a subset of the other. Write the proof in two clearly labeled directions.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Recall that \(x \in A \setminus (B \cap C)\) means \(x \in A\) and \(x \notin B \cap C\text{.}\) The statement \(x \notin B \cap C\) means it is not the case that (\(x \in B\) and \(x \in C\)). By De Morgan’s law for logic, this is equivalent to: \(x \notin B\) or \(x \notin C\text{.}\) Now consider the two cases.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Part 2: Proof Choice (Required — Choose One) (Estimated time: 20 minutes)
    🔗
    Choose one of the following two proof problems. Both ask for a complete proof; they differ in difficulty and the techniques required. Label your submission clearly with “Option A” or “Option B.”
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.2. Option A (Standard): Multiples of 3 Are Countably Infinite.

    Let \(T = \{3n : n \in \Z\}\) be the set of all integer multiples of 3.
    1. Construct an explicit bijection \(f : \N \to T\text{.}\) (Use the bijection \(\N \to \Z\) from Worked Example 4 as a guide, or construct a direct bijection by interleaving positive and negative multiples of 3.)
      🔗
    2. Prove that your function is injective.
      🔗
    3. Prove that your function is surjective.
      🔗
    4. Conclude that \(T\) is countably infinite.
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    One approach: define \(g : \N \to T\) by \(g(n) = 3 \cdot f(n)\text{,}\) where \(f : \N \to \Z\) is the bijection from Worked Example 4. Then verify that \(g\) is a bijection \(\N \to T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.3. Option B (More Challenging): Countable Union of Countable Sets.

    Let \(A\) and \(B\) be countable sets. Prove that \(A \cup B\) is countable.
    🔗
    You may use the following facts without proof: \(\N \times \{0,1\}\) is countably infinite (since it is in bijection with \(\N\)), and any subset of a countable set is finite or countably infinite (Zorn Proposition 1.17). You may not invoke Proposition 1.19 (Zorn) directly, as that is the general version of the result you are proving.
    🔗
    Your proof should:
    1. Handle the case where at least one of \(A\) or \(B\) is finite separately, or explain why it suffices to assume both are countably infinite.
      🔗
    2. Construct an explicit surjection from \(\N\) onto \(A \cup B\text{,}\) or an explicit injection from \(A \cup B\) into a known countable set.
      🔗
    3. Use Proposition 1.17 to conclude.
      🔗
    🔗
    Hint.
    Since \(A\) and \(B\) are countably infinite, there exist surjections (or bijections) \(a : \N \to A\) and \(b : \N \to B\text{.}\) Define \(h : \N \to A \cup B\) by sending even \(n\) to \(a(n/2)\) and odd \(n\) to \(b((n-1)/2)\text{.}\) Show \(h\) is a surjection and apply Proposition 1.17.
    🔗

    Part 3: Reflection (Estimated time: 5 minutes)
    🔗
    Write 3–5 sentences for each question. These are graded for thoughtfulness, not for mathematical correctness.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.4. Bauldry §2.1 in Set-Theoretic Language.

    Identify one place in Bauldry §2.1 where a set-theoretic argument from Module 3 (membership, subset, union, intersection, set difference, or element-chasing) is used. Describe in 2–3 sentences what the argument is and why the set operation involved matters in that context.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.5. Element-Chasing as a Proof Template.

    In Part 1 and Worked Example 1, you used the element-chasing method. Describe the template in your own words: what is the general structure of an element-chasing proof of a set equality? Then reflect: was there a step in your Part 1 proof where you had to make a choice about how to proceed? What guided that choice?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 6.6. Looking Ahead.

    In Module 4, you will prove convergence of sequences using \(\varepsilon\)-\(N\) arguments. A sequence is a function \(a : \N \to \R\text{.}\) Based on what you learned in Module 3 about functions, injectivity, and countability, describe one connection you see between the material of Module 3 and the idea of a convergent sequence. What properties of the function \(a\) (if any) are relevant to whether the sequence converges?
